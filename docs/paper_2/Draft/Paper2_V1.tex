This section specifies the optimisation model. Section~\ref{sec:model:framework}
delimits what is inherited from the dispatch formulation of Paper~1
\cite{Ruess.2026} and what is added here; Sections~\ref{sec:model:balances}
to~\ref{sec:model:tes} present the physical and technological
constraints; Section~\ref{sec:model:objective} states the investment
layer, the objective function and the two linearisation techniques
employed; and Section~\ref{sec:model:validation} sets out the validation
strategy. \Cref{fig:F1} summarises the model architecture.

\subsection{Modelling framework and fidelity level}
\label{sec:model:framework}

The model is implemented in the CALION framework and operates at the
fidelity level denoted $\Lplus$ in Paper~1 \cite{Ruess.2026}: a
node-and-pipe representation of the physical network in which supply
temperatures propagate spatially, thermal losses depend on the local
temperature level, pressure drop is represented by a linearised
Darcy--Weisbach relation, and transport delay is resolved at hourly
granularity. Paper~1 established this level by systematically comparing
five variants, from a copperplate abstraction without network physics to
a mixed-integer quadratically constrained formulation without
linearisation, and quantified both the topology-abstraction error and
the linearisation error incurred at $\Lplus$. That comparison is not
repeated here.

The present work takes $\Lplus$ as given and adds an investment and
siting layer on top of it. Concretely, the following elements are
inherited unchanged and are therefore stated only to the extent needed
for the model to be readable in isolation: the hydraulic representation,
the temperature-dependent loss propagation along pipes, the transport
delay formulation, and the calibration of the two networks against
metered operational data. The following elements are new: the discrete
capacity selection for thermal storage, the siting variables and their
colocation variants, the electrode boiler and heat pump investment
decisions, the network-transformation stages described in
Section~\ref{sec:model:heatcurve}, and the nested
enumeration--optimization scheme that links them.

A note on terminology is warranted. In what follows, an
\emph{investment variable} is a quantity the solver chooses within a
single optimisation run, whereas a \emph{stage} is a member of an
enumerated set over which independent runs are performed. Heat pump,
electrode boiler and storage capacity as well as siting are investment
variables. The network transformation stage is not.

\subsection{Nodal energy and mass balances}
\label{sec:model:balances}

For every node $i$ and hour $t$, thermal power is conserved:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:heatbalance}
\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i} q_{a,i}(t) + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i^{-}} \dot{Q}_{ji}(t)
= D_i(t) + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i^{+}} \dot{Q}_{ik}(t)
+ q_i^{\mathrm{ch}}(t) - q_i^{\mathrm{dis}}(t) + Q_i^{\mathrm{dump}}(t),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the set of generation assets at node $i$,
$\mathcal{N}_i^{-}$ and $\mathcal{N}_i^{+}$ are the sets of nodes
feeding into and fed from node~$i$, $D_i$ is heat demand,
$q_i^{\mathrm{ch}}$ and $q_i^{\mathrm{dis}}$ are storage charging and
discharging power, and $Q_i^{\mathrm{dump}}$ is a curtailment slack that
absorbs surplus waste heat. Network losses are embedded in the delivered
power $\dot{Q}_{jk}$ of each pipe rather than appearing as a separate
term.

Independently of \cref{eq:heatbalance}, water mass is conserved:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:massbalance}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i^{-}} \dot{m}_{ji}(t) + \dot{m}_i^{\mathrm{gen}}(t)
= \sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i^{+}} \dot{m}_{ik}(t) + \dot{m}_i^{\mathrm{dem}}(t),
\qquad
\dot{m}_i^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = \frac{10^{3}\, q_i(t)}{c_p \left(T_{\mathrm{s},i} - T_{\mathrm{r},i}\right)} .
\end{equation}
Maintaining \cref{eq:massbalance} as a constraint in its own right,
rather than treating mass flow as a post-processed image of delivered
heat, matters at nodes that simultaneously host local generation and
pass flow downstream. At such nodes both the generation term and the
downstream delivery terms enter \cref{eq:heatbalance}, and their
interaction with \cref{eq:massbalance} is the point at which a
formulation that conserves energy globally can nevertheless violate mass
continuity locally. Both networks studied here contain such nodes.

\subsection{Network transformation stages and the heat curve}
\label{sec:model:heatcurve}

Supply temperature is governed by an ambient-compensated heat curve of
the standard parameterised form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:heatcurve}
T_{\mathrm{s}}(t) = \min\!\left\{ T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\max},\,
\max\!\left\{ T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min},\;
T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min} + \kHC \left(T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\max} - T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min}\right)
\frac{T_{\mathrm{h}} - T_{\mathrm{a}}(t)}{T_{\mathrm{h}} - T_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{des}}}
\right\} \right\},
\end{equation}
with heating limit temperature $T_{\mathrm{h}} = \SI{15}{\celsius}$,
design ambient temperature $T_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{des}} =
\SI{-12}{\celsius}$ -- both following DIN~EN~12831 and identical for the
two networks -- and slope parameter $\kHC$. Heat curve control of this type is standard practice and its
optimisation has been studied for individual heat pump installations
\cite{Krug.2025}; what is specific here is that the curve is applied at
network level and interacts with siting and capacity decisions.

Three stages, denoted HK0, HK1 and HK2, are considered. It is important
to be precise about what distinguishes them, because they are not three
values of a single parameter. Each stage bundles a slope $\kHC$, a
minimum supply temperature $T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min}$ and, for the retrofit
stages, a reduced return temperature $T_{\mathrm{r}}$
(\cref{tab:T2}). HK0 ($\kHC = 1.0$; $T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min} =
\SI{70}{\celsius}$ and \SI{74}{\celsius} for the two networks)
represents the existing building stock. HK1 ($\kHC = 0.8$;
\SI{65}{\celsius} and \SI{70}{\celsius}) represents retrofitting half of
the substations, which lowers the achievable return temperature. HK2
($\kHC = 0.6$; \SI{60}{\celsius} and \SI{66}{\celsius}) represents
complete substation retrofit. A fourth setting, denoted TVLFIX, holds
the supply temperature at its historical constant value and serves as
the reference against which heat curve control is assessed.

The stages are therefore alternative network-transformation narratives
rather than samples of a continuously adjustable design lever. This
distinction is not cosmetic: a continuous $\kHC$ would leave
$T_{\mathrm{s}}^{\min}$ and $T_{\mathrm{r}}$ undefined, since neither
follows from the slope, and both are consequences of a discrete
investment programme in the building stock. Treating the transformation
stage as an enumerated dimension is thus the modelling choice that
matches the underlying decision structure. It has the secondary benefit
of keeping $T_{\mathrm{s}}(t)$ exogenous within each run, which
preserves linearity in the constraints of
Sections~\ref{sec:model:hp} and~\ref{sec:model:tes}.

\subsection{Heat pump and electrode boiler models}
\label{sec:model:hp}

Heat pump electricity consumption is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pel}
P^{\mathrm{el}}(t) = \frac{\dot{Q}^{\mathrm{HR}}(t)}{\COP(t)}
 + \frac{\dot{Q}^{\mathrm{def}}(t)}{\COP^{\mathrm{def}}},
\end{equation}
where $\dot{Q}^{\mathrm{HR}}$ is heat recovered from the industrial
waste heat source and $\dot{Q}^{\mathrm{def}}$ is the share supplied at
a default performance figure when the source is unavailable. The
coefficient of performance follows a Lorenz-type correlation evaluated
on logarithmic mean temperatures on both the sink and the source side,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cop}
\COP(t) = A(t)\, B(t)\, \eta \left(1 - q_{\mathrm{ww}}(t)\right)
+ 1 - \eta - F_Q ,
\end{equation}
where $A(t)$ is the reciprocal Lorenz efficiency formed from the two
logarithmic mean temperatures, $B(t)$ corrects for pinch and internal
temperature losses, and $q_{\mathrm{ww}}$ and $F_Q$ are empirical loss
terms. The correlation assumes a source-side spread of \SI{20}{\kelvin}
and a pinch of \SI{5}{\kelvin} on both heat exchangers, and the result is
clipped to $[1.01, 12]$. It is an internally calibrated correlation
rather than one adopted from the literature; it is therefore stated in
full in \ref{app:cop} so that the results remain reproducible. Simpler
Lorenz surrogates, and their accuracy relative to detailed cycle models,
have been compared systematically for energy-planning applications
\cite{Pieper.2020}.
\placeholder{Resolve the exergetic efficiency before submission: the
configurations currently use eta = 0.75 for one network and eta = 0.6 for
the other, while Paper~1 documents 0.6 with a simpler equation. If the
values are not harmonised, the difference must be justified here and
carried into the limitations.}

Two properties of \cref{eq:cop} govern how it enters the optimisation.
First, the exergetic efficiency $\eta$ is a scalar; the temperature
dependence of $\COP(t)$ arises entirely from the source and sink
temperature series. Second, and more consequentially, $\COP(t)$ is
evaluated \emph{outside} the optimisation, once per transformation
stage, from the exogenous supply temperature series produced by
\cref{eq:heatcurve}, and is passed to the solver as a fixed parameter.
The performance figure is thus consistently propagated into every stage
but is not itself a decision variable, and \cref{eq:pel} remains linear.
We state this explicitly because the coupling between supply
temperature and heat pump performance is central to the results in
Section~\ref{sec:results:coupling}, and that coupling is a modelled
physical consequence of the enumerated stage, not an outcome the solver
negotiates.

Heat pump operation is subject to a commitment binary $u(t)$ and a
minimum part load, giving the standard product of a binary with a
bounded continuous capacity that is treated in
Section~\ref{sec:model:objective}. The electrode boiler is modelled as a
continuous investment capacity
$\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{EB}} \in [0, \overline{Q}_{\mathrm{EB}}]$ with
conversion efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{EB}}$ (\num{0.98} and \num{0.95}
for the two networks) and is available in every non-baseline scenario.
It is always sited at the same node as the heat pump; its capacity is
consequently a result rather than a scenario dimension, which is why it
does not appear as a column in the scenario matrix. One network
additionally contains an existing \SI{9.9}{\mega\watt} power-to-heat
boiler at the same node. It carries no investment decision:
there is no build binary and no annualised capital cost attached to it,
only variable operating cost. It is therefore complementary to, and
independent of, the investable electrode boiler.

\subsection{Thermal energy storage model}
\label{sec:model:tes}

Storage capacity is selected from a discrete ladder rather than sized
continuously. The ladder is anchored to hours of mean network load,
$\{0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 36, 48, 96, 168\}$ hours. For the larger
network, whose mean load is about \SI{73}{\mega\watt}, this yields steps
of $0, 73, 146, \ldots, \SI{845}{\mega\watt\hour}$, truncated at
approximately twelve hours of mean load for numerical tractability; for
the smaller network, with a mean load of about \SI{1.4}{\mega\watt}, the
full ladder is retained and reaches \SI{235}{\mega\watt\hour}. Anchoring
the ladder to load rather than to catalogue vessel sizes makes the two
cases directly comparable despite a difference in scale of more than a
factor of fifty.

Selection is exact:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tesladder}
E^{\max} = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} E_s\, z_s,
\qquad \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} z_s = 1,
\qquad z_s \in \{0,1\},
\end{equation}
so that capacity is linear in the selection binaries and no big-M
coupling is required. This formulation replaced an earlier one in which
volume was continuous and linked to an activation binary through a big-M
constraint. The replacement is reported here because it is a
transferable modelling result rather than an implementation detail: the
weak linear relaxation of the big-M variant left precisely those
scenarios that place storage at a consumer node stalled at MIP gaps
between \SI{43}{\percent} and \SI{88}{\percent}, and these are the
scenarios that turn out to carry the strongest economic effect
(Section~\ref{sec:results:siting}). A formulation choice that appears
innocuous can therefore suppress the very result the study is designed
to find.

Energy capacity is converted to volume through a stage-specific
coefficient $\xi(\mathrm{HK})$ that depends on the temperature spread
available at that transformation stage, $V = E^{\max} / \xi(\mathrm{HK})$.
An immediate consequence, and one that is easily overlooked, is that the
same rung of the energy ladder corresponds to a different physical
vessel volume at each stage: lowering the supply temperature reduces the
usable spread and therefore inflates the volume, and hence the capital
cost, required to store a given quantity of heat. This is a direct
coupling between the transformation stage and the storage investment,
and it operates in the opposite direction to the efficiency gain that
the same stage confers on the heat pump. The two effects are what the
co-design question in this paper is ultimately about.

Volumes exceeding a standard unit vessel of \SI{25000}{\cubic\metre} are
realised as $N = \lceil V / V_{\mathrm{unit}} \rceil$ identical tanks,
with the fixed cost incurred per tank rather than per capacity step;
this preserves the economies of scale of tank storage while respecting
constructional limits \cite{Dahash.2021}. The multi-tank case arises only
in the larger network; every rung of the smaller network's ladder fits
within a single vessel. The state of charge evolves as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:soc}
E(t) = \lambda^{\Delta t} E(t-1)
 + \eta^{\mathrm{ch}} q^{\mathrm{ch}}(t)\,\Delta t
 - \frac{q^{\mathrm{dis}}(t)\,\Delta t}{\eta^{\mathrm{dis}}},
\qquad 0 \le E(t) \le E^{\max},
\end{equation}
with hourly retention factor $\lambda = \num{0.9999}$, corresponding to a
self-discharge of \SI{0.01}{\percent} per hour, and charging and
discharging efficiencies of \num{0.98} in both networks. The initial
state of charge is fixed at half the installed capacity, and a terminal
state of charge of at least half the installed capacity is enforced as an
inequality. This prevents end-of-horizon depletion without imposing
strict cyclicity: the storage is permitted to finish the year fuller than
it began, but not emptier.

Siting is treated in three variants. In the fixed variants the storage
node is prescribed from a set of candidate nodes; in the endogenous
variant a binary siting vector is optimised over the same candidate set;
and in the colocation variant storage is constrained to share the node
of the heat pump. Comparing the three is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:results:siting}.

\subsection{Investment layer, objective function and linearisation}
\label{sec:model:objective}

Capital expenditure is annualised following VDI~2067,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:annuity}
\mathrm{ANF}(i,n) = \frac{i\,(1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n} - 1},
\end{equation}
with discount rate $i = \num{0.05}$ and component lifetimes of
\num{20}, \num{25} and \num{30} years for the heat pump, the electrode
boiler and the storage respectively. Investment cost is linear with a
fixed activation component,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:capex}
\mathrm{CAPEX} = \alpha_{\mathrm{HP}} \QWP + \beta_{\mathrm{HP}} y_{\mathrm{HP}}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{EB}} \dot{Q}_{\mathrm{EB}} + \beta_{\mathrm{EB}} y_{\mathrm{EB}}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{TES}} V + \beta_{\mathrm{TES}} N,
\end{equation}
and the objective is total annualised cost,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tac}
\TAC = \sum_{c} \mathrm{ANF}(i, n_c)\, \mathrm{CAPEX}_c + \mathrm{OPEX},
\end{equation}
where OPEX comprises electricity procurement including the demand
charge, fuel, carbon cost, and a curtailment penalty on
$Q^{\mathrm{dump}}$.

Two distinct linearisation techniques appear in the formulation, and
conflating them would misstate the model's error properties.

The first is a McCormick relaxation \cite{Castro.2015} of the bilinear
products of a mass flow with a temperature, $W = \dot{m}\, c_p\, T$,
both factors being continuous variables. For bounds
$\dot{m} \in [\underline{m}, \overline{m}]$ and
$T \in [\underline{T}, \overline{T}]$ the convex hull is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mccormick}
\begin{aligned}
W &\ge c_p\left(\underline{m} T + \dot{m}\underline{T} - \underline{m}\,\underline{T}\right), &
W &\ge c_p\left(\overline{m} T + \dot{m}\overline{T} - \overline{m}\,\overline{T}\right), \\
W &\le c_p\left(\overline{m} T + \dot{m}\underline{T} - \overline{m}\,\underline{T}\right), &
W &\le c_p\left(\underline{m} T + \dot{m}\overline{T} - \underline{m}\,\overline{T}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Seven such products occur: pipe inlet and outlet enthalpy, mixing at
nodes with more than one incoming pipe, consumer withdrawal on the
supply and on the return side, and local generation feed-in on the
supply and on the return side. \Cref{eq:mccormick} is an outer
approximation and therefore introduces a relaxation gap, quantified in
Paper~1 \cite{Ruess.2026}. Two of the seven are inactive in the present
study: both networks are tree topologies, so no node receives more than
one incoming pipe and the mixing constraint is never binding, and return
temperature is a stage-fixed parameter rather than a variable in both
configurations. We report them nonetheless, since the constraints are
part of the formulation and would activate on a meshed topology.

The second technique concerns the product of a commitment binary with a
bounded continuous capacity, $\QWP u(t)$. This is reformulated exactly,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bigm}
q(t) \le \overline{Q}\, u(t), \quad
q(t) \le \QWP, \quad
q(t) \ge \QWP - \overline{Q}\left(1 - u(t)\right), \quad
q(t) \ge 0,
\end{equation}
which is tight at every integer-feasible point and contributes no
relaxation error. Two further products are not bilinear at all and
require no treatment: $\dot{Q}/\COP$ in \cref{eq:pel}, because $\COP$ is
a parameter, and the energy-to-volume conversion in
Section~\ref{sec:model:tes}, because the temperature spread is fixed
within a stage.

One departure from the $\Lplus$ formulation of Paper~1 was necessary for
tractability. Applying full McCormick temperature propagation across all
\num{8760} hours proved computationally intractable for the smaller
network at the scenario counts required here; a linear supply
temperature offset per pipe is used in its place. The consequences for
the spatial temperature profile are examined in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\subsection{Validation strategy}
\label{sec:model:validation}

Five checks are applied and reported in \cref{tab:T5}.

Energy conservation is verified by a per-node audit that reads the
solved state directly and compares total heat entering each node against
demand plus onward delivery. Agreement is within
\SI{0.04}{\percent}. We report this rather than an aggregate
generation-plus-net-storage-against-demand statistic, because the latter
is not a valid conservation test in the presence of storage that cycles
within the hour: netting charge against discharge collapses the
throughput of a storage unit that has moved substantial energy in both
directions to approximately zero, and the resulting apparent residual
reflects the arithmetic of the test, not a violation of
\cref{eq:heatbalance}. The distinction matters here because the
best-performing configuration is precisely one in which the storage
cycles in the great majority of hours.

Solver convergence is reported as gap statistics over the
\num{46} canonical scenario runs. Auxiliary runs used to enumerate
siting pairs are excluded from this population and reported separately,
since they are deliberately solved to a fixed time budget rather than to
proven optimality.

Consistency with Paper~1 is established through a dedicated validation
run in which heat pump and electrode boiler capacities are held at the
values used there, reproducing the operating cost of the reference case.
This run is a validation instrument only and is not part of the
\num{46}-scenario matrix.

Two plausibility checks complete the set: annual mean coefficients of
performance are required to fall within a physically defensible band for
the temperature lifts involved, and the discrete optimum is required to
coincide, within one grid step and \SI{10}{\percent} in total annualised
cost, with the minimum of the continuous capacity surface obtained from
the parametric sweep of Section~\ref{sec:results:sweep}.

\section{Coefficient of performance correlation}
\label{app:cop}

The coefficient of performance in \cref{eq:cop} is evaluated from the
supply temperature series of the respective transformation stage. With
the logarithmic mean temperature
\begin{equation}
L(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{c}})
= \frac{T_{\mathrm{h}} - T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\ln (T_{\mathrm{h}}/T_{\mathrm{c}})},
\end{equation}
the source outlet temperature
$T_{\mathrm{src,out}}(t) = \max\{T_{\mathrm{src}}(t) - \Delta T,\, 1\}$
and the sink outlet temperature
$T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t) = T_{\mathrm{s}}(t)$ from
\cref{eq:heatcurve}, define
\begin{align}
L_{\mathrm{sink}}(t) &= L\!\left(T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t),\, T_{\mathrm{sink,in}}\right), &
L_{\mathrm{src}}(t) &= L\!\left(T_{\mathrm{src}}(t),\, T_{\mathrm{src,out}}(t)\right).
\end{align}
Two empirical loss terms account for internal temperature differences and
auxiliary consumption,
\begin{align}
m_{\mathrm{dts}}(t) &= 0.2\left(T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{src,out}}(t) + 2\Delta T_{\mathrm{pp}}\right)
 + 0.2\left(T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{sink,in}}\right) + 0.016, \\
q_{\mathrm{ww}}(t) &= 0.0014\left(T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{src,out}}(t) + 2\Delta T_{\mathrm{pp}}\right)
 - 0.0015\left(T_{\mathrm{sink,out}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{sink,in}}\right) + 0.039,
\end{align}
from which the two factors of \cref{eq:cop} follow as
\begin{equation}
A(t) = \frac{L_{\mathrm{sink}}(t)}{L_{\mathrm{sink}}(t) - L_{\mathrm{src}}(t)},
\qquad
B(t) = \frac{1 + \dfrac{m_{\mathrm{dts}}(t) + \Delta T_{\mathrm{pp}}}{L_{\mathrm{sink}}(t)}}
{1 + \dfrac{m_{\mathrm{dts}}(t) + 0.5\left(T_{\mathrm{src}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{src,out}}(t)\right) + 2\Delta T_{\mathrm{pp}}}
{L_{\mathrm{sink}}(t) - L_{\mathrm{src}}(t)}} .
\end{equation}
The result is clipped to the interval $[1.01, 12]$. Parameter values are
$F_Q = \num{0.10}$, $\Delta T = \SI{20}{\kelvin}$ and
$\Delta T_{\mathrm{pp}} = \SI{5}{\kelvin}$ for both networks;
$T_{\mathrm{sink,in}}$ and $\eta$ are network specific and are listed in
\cref{tab:T1}.
