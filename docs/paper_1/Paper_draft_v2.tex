\begin{abstract}
District heating network planning studies require practitioners to
choose both a topology representation level and a physics fidelity
level, yet no quantitative guidance exists for either decision in
isolation. Prior comparisons confound topology abstraction with
physics model changes, and studies employing nonlinear hydraulic
models have not been benchmarked against linearized MILP equivalents
under controlled conditions.

This paper presents the first controlled comparison that
systematically varies both topology and physics fidelity across five
model variants within a unified framework: L1 (copperplate, no
network physics), L2 (simplified multi-node with steady-state thermal
losses), L3 (detailed multi-node MILP with identical steady-state
losses), $\Lplus$ (detailed multi-node MILP with linearized pressure
drop, temperature-dependent loss propagation, and transport delay),
and $\Lnl$ (detailed multi-node with identical extended physics
modeled as a mixed-integer quadratically constrained program without
linearization). This design yields three orthogonal comparisons:
topology effect (L1$\to$L2$\to$L3), physics fidelity effect
(L3$\to\Lplus$), and linearization error ($\Lplus\to\Lnl$). The
framework is validated against measured operational data from a
German industrial heat network comprising heat pumps, thermal
storage, a CHP plant, and grid electricity coupling.
Generalizability is assessed across 36 synthetic network
configurations spanning systematic variations in pipe length, demand
heterogeneity, node count, and storage capacity.

Results reveal a hierarchy of effects: the L1$\to$L2 transition
(physics inclusion) drives cost differences of \placeholder{X}\%
(\placeholder{€Y,000}/year) and \ce{CO2} differences of
\placeholder{Z}~t/year; the L3$\to\Lplus$ transition (extended
physics) adds \placeholder{X--Y}\% through pressure-drop-driven
pumping costs and transport-delay-induced dispatch shifts; while the
$\Lplus\to\Lnl$ comparison (linearization error) reveals that
piecewise linearization introduces a cost deviation of only
\placeholder{X.X}\%, validating the MILP approximation for planning
purposes. Thermal storage dispatch emerges as the primary diagnostic
variable: L1 shifts optimal charge timing by \placeholder{X}~hours
(loss ignorance), while $\Lplus$ shifts discharge timing by
\placeholder{X.X}~hours relative to L3 (transport delay effect).
The $\Lnl$ model serves as a fidelity benchmark, confirming that
$\Lplus$ captures \placeholder{XX}\% of the extended-physics effect
at \placeholder{X}$\times$ lower solve time.

These findings yield a five-level decision framework: L1 suffices
for initial screening; L2 is the minimum for storage-containing
systems; L3 balances fidelity and tractability for most planning
studies; $\Lplus$ is warranted when pumping costs exceed
\placeholder{X}\% of thermal losses or transport times exceed
\placeholder{X}~h; and $\Lnl$ serves as a validation benchmark
for linearization adequacy.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:motivation}

Space and process heat account for approximately 50\% of final energy
consumption in the European Union, of which roughly 60\% is still
supplied by fossil fuels~\cite{EUheating2016}. District heating (DH)
networks offer a systemic decarbonization pathway through sector
coupling: large-scale heat pumps, combined heat and power (CHP) units,
thermal energy storage, and electrode boilers can be coordinated within
a single network~\cite{Lund2014_4GDH,Werner2017}. Planning and
operational optimization of such multi-source networks increasingly
relies on mathematical programming due to its optimality guarantees
and tractable handling of commitment decisions.

Every optimization-based planning study, however, confronts two
fundamental and interrelated modeling choices that have received
remarkably little systematic attention:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Topology abstraction:} the level of spatial detail
    at which the network is represented---from a single-bus
    copperplate to a pipe-by-pipe graph;
    \item \textbf{Physics fidelity:} the set of physical phenomena
    modeled---from zero-loss energy balances to pressure-drop-coupled,
    temperature-propagating, delay-aware thermo-hydraulic models.
\end{enumerate}

At one extreme, \emph{copperplate} models aggregate all components
and demands to a single bus, ignoring spatial structure and thermal
losses entirely. At the other extreme, \emph{full thermo-hydraulic}
models resolve the physical pipe-by-pipe network, capturing thermal
losses (5--15\% of annual heat supply~\cite{Frederiksen2013,Talebi2016}),
pressure-dependent pumping costs, temperature propagation through the
network, and transport delays arising from finite fluid velocity. The
practical consequences of these choices affect cost estimation,
dispatch quality, and \ce{CO2} accounting simultaneously---yet
practitioners currently lack quantitative guidance on the
accuracy--tractability trade-off across both dimensions.

A further complication arises from the \emph{mathematical formulation}:
extended thermo-hydraulic physics introduce nonlinear and bilinear
terms (pressure drop is quadratic in flow; temperature losses
involve flow--temperature products) that are incompatible with
standard MILP solvers. Practitioners must choose between
linearization (preserving MILP tractability but introducing
approximation error) and nonlinear/quadratic formulations (preserving
physical accuracy but sacrificing global optimality guarantees and
potentially increasing solve times). The magnitude of the
linearization error in a DH dispatch context has not been quantified.

\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The core problem is \emph{multi-dimensional confounding}: published
comparisons between different topology and physics representations
change multiple modeling dimensions simultaneously, making it
impossible to attribute observed differences to any single factor.
For instance, comparing the full nonlinear pipe model with
temperature-dependent COP used by Vesterlund et
al.~\cite{Vesterlund2017} against the copperplate formulation with
constant thermal efficiencies of Gabrielli et
al.~\cite{Gabrielli2018} conflates topology effects with
(1)~NLP vs.\ constant-loss formulations, (2)~temperature-dependent
vs.\ constant COP, (3)~the presence or absence of hydraulic
coupling, and (4)~potentially differing temporal resolutions. The
same pattern appears systematically across the literature
(\Cref{tab:prior_work} in \Cref{sec:related_work}).

Four specific gaps persist:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item No study isolates topology abstraction as the sole
    experimental variable while holding physics constant.
    \item No study isolates the physics fidelity effect (adding
    pressure drop, temperature propagation, transport delay) while
    holding topology constant.
    \item The linearization error introduced by MILP-compatible
    approximations of thermo-hydraulic physics has not been
    quantified against a quadratic reference in a DH dispatch
    context.
    \item No quantitative guidance exists for joint topology and
    physics selection as a function of observable network properties.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions and Research Questions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This paper addresses these gaps through five contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Five-level taxonomy}: A reusable classification
    framework for DH modeling fidelity---L1 (copperplate), L2
    (simplified multi-node), L3 (detailed MILP, basic physics),
    $\Lplus$ (detailed MILP, extended physics), $\Lnl$ (detailed
    quadratic, extended physics)---enabling controlled comparison
    across both topology and physics dimensions
    (\Cref{sec:topology_def}).

    \item \textbf{First controlled topology comparison}: Three
    topology levels (L1/L2/L3) embedded within a unified MILP
    framework where L2 and L3 share identical physics, validated
    against measured industrial operational data (\Cref{sec:results_cost}).

    \item \textbf{First controlled physics fidelity comparison}:
    L3 vs.\ $\Lplus$ isolates the effect of adding pressure drop,
    temperature-dependent loss propagation, and transport delay while
    holding topology constant (\Cref{sec:results_extended}).

    \item \textbf{Linearization error quantification}: $\Lplus$
    (MILP with piecewise-linear and Big-M approximations) vs.\
    $\Lnl$ (MIQCP with native quadratic constraints) quantifies
    the cost of linearization (\Cref{sec:results_linearization}).

    \item \textbf{Actionable decision criteria}: Parametric analysis
    across 36 synthetic configurations yielding joint
    topology-and-physics selection rules based on observable network
    properties (\Cref{sec:results_generalizability}).
\end{enumerate}

These contributions answer four research questions:
\begin{description}[style=nextline,nosep]
    \item[RQ1 --- Topology Effect:]
    How much does topology abstraction change annual operational cost
    and \ce{CO2} emissions when physics are held constant?
    (L1 vs.\ L2 vs.\ L3)

    \item[RQ2 --- Physics Fidelity Effect:]
    How much do pressure drop, temperature-dependent losses, and
    transport delay change dispatch and cost when topology is held
    constant? (L3 vs.\ $\Lplus$)

    \item[RQ3 --- Linearization Error:]
    How large is the cost deviation introduced by MILP-compatible
    linearization of thermo-hydraulic physics compared to a
    quadratic reference? ($\Lplus$ vs.\ $\Lnl$)

    \item[RQ4 --- Generalizability:]
    Which network properties determine whether detailed topology
    and/or extended physics representation is warranted?
\end{description}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{MILP Optimization of District Heating Systems}
\label{sec:rw_milp}

MILP has become the dominant methodology for operational optimization
of district heating systems~\cite{Fang2016,Guelpa2019review}. Several
open-source frameworks support multi-carrier energy system optimization:
oemof~\cite{Hilpert2018_oemof} with the oemof-thermal
extension~\cite{Krien2020_oemofthermal},
PyPSA~\cite{Brown2018_pypsa}, FINE~\cite{Welder2018_FINE},
Calliope~\cite{Pfenninger2018_calliope}, urbs~\cite{Dorfner2016_urbs},
and Balmorel~\cite{Wiese2018_balmorel}. Despite their diversity, these
frameworks share common limitations for detailed DH analysis: thermal
losses are typically time-invariant, heat pump COP is constant or
seasonal, hydraulic coupling is absent, and network topology is either
fully aggregated or fixed at a single detail level.

In the multi-energy system (MES) literature,
Mancarella~\cite{Mancarella2014} established the theoretical basis
for multi-energy hub modeling, while Gabrielli et
al.~\cite{Gabrielli2018} formulated a MILP for distributed MES
including thermal and electrical storage with hourly resolution.
These studies optimize component dispatch and sizing but treat the
thermal network as a single aggregated efficiency factor---a
simplification whose cost this paper quantifies.

\subsection{Topology Representation Spectrum}
\label{sec:rw_topology}

The DH modeling literature exhibits a continuous spectrum of spatial
aggregation, which we organize into categories corresponding to our
taxonomy.

\textbf{Single-node aggregation (L1-equivalent):} Standard in
national-scale energy system models where heating is one of many
sectors~\cite{Connolly2014_EnergyPLAN,Brown2018_pypsa,
Lund2017_SmartEnergy}. No spatial structure or thermal losses.

\textbf{Zone-based models (L2-equivalent):} Demand aggregated into
geographic zones (3--15 nodes) connected by simplified pipe
segments~\cite{Wirtz2020,Mertz2016,Buffa2019}. Bünning et
al.~\cite{Buenning2020} demonstrate that zone-level aggregation
captures 85--95\% of dynamic temperature variation.

\textbf{Full-graph models (L3-equivalent):} Node-by-node
representation with pipe-by-pipe parameters from GIS
data~\cite{Vesterlund2017,Schweiger2017,vanderHeijde2017,
Blommaert2020}. Giraud et al.~\cite{Giraud2015} show tractability
up to $\approx$100 nodes for hourly annual horizons.

\subsection{Thermo-Hydraulic Network Models}
\label{sec:rw_thermohydraulic}

Beyond topology, the physics fidelity of pipe models varies
substantially across the literature.

\textbf{Steady-state thermal losses:} The simplest physically
meaningful model, where pipe losses follow from the
fluid--ground temperature difference and pipe insulation
properties~\cite{Paalsson1999,Frederiksen2013}. MILP-compatible
when temperatures are fixed parameters.

\textbf{Pressure drop and pumping:} The Darcy--Weisbach equation
relates pressure drop to the square of flow velocity:
$\Delta p = f_D \frac{L}{D} \frac{\rho v^2}{2}$, introducing a
quadratic nonlinearity. Pumping power is proportional to
$\dot{m} \cdot \Delta p$, creating a cubic term in mass flow.
Several authors have addressed this through piecewise
linearization~\cite{Bordin2016,Mertz2016} or second-order cone
relaxations~\cite{Blommaert2020}. Reported pumping costs range
from 1--5\% of thermal energy costs depending on network length
and flow velocity~\cite{Frederiksen2013}.

\textbf{Temperature propagation:} In physical networks, heat
losses along a pipe reduce the temperature at downstream nodes.
This creates a coupling between upstream generation decisions and
downstream deliverable temperatures. The temperature drop along
a pipe of length~$L$ under steady-state conditions
follows~\cite{vanderHeijde2017}:
$T_{\mathrm{out}} = T_{\mathrm{ground}} +
(T_{\mathrm{in}} - T_{\mathrm{ground}})
\exp(-U L / (\dot{m} c_p))$,
introducing a nonlinear (exponential) dependence on mass flow.
When linearized, this enters MILP models as a temperature reduction
parameter per pipe segment.

\textbf{Transport delay:} Heat injected at the source reaches
downstream consumers after a delay $\tau = L / v$, where $v$ is
the flow velocity. For typical DH networks, transport times range
from minutes (short branches) to 1--3~hours (long trunk
lines)~\cite{vanderHeijde2017,Giraud2015}. Delays are commonly
modeled via the ``node method'' or ``plug-flow''
discretization~\cite{Benonysson1995,Gabrielaitiene2007}, which
shift heat injections in time. In MILP formulations, fixed delays
(computed from nominal flow velocity) introduce additional
temporal coupling constraints.

\textbf{Nonlinear and quadratic formulations:}
Vesterlund et al.~\cite{Vesterlund2017} formulated a full NLP for
multi-source DH optimization with temperature-dependent losses.
Blommaert et al.~\cite{Blommaert2020} used adjoint-based NLP for
topology design. Schweiger et al.~\cite{Schweiger2017} employed
Modelica co-simulation for dynamic thermo-hydraulic coupling.
Mixed-integer quadratically constrained programs (MIQCP) offer a
middle ground: quadratic constraints (e.g., Darcy--Weisbach) are
handled natively by modern solvers (Gurobi, CPLEX) without
piecewise linearization, while integer variables retain unit
commitment logic~\cite{Ommen2016}. However, MIQCP problems lack
the LP relaxation tightness of MILP, potentially increasing solve
times and reducing optimality guarantees for large instances.

Notably, Leitner et al.~\cite{Leitner2019} compared copperplate and
full-graph representations for an Austrian DH network and found cost
differences of 3--8\%, but simultaneously changed the loss physics
between levels. Volkova et al.~\cite{Volkova2018} studied
topology-dependent losses in Estonian networks, confirming that pipe
routing significantly affects loss estimates---but in a simulation
(not optimization) context. Moser et al.~\cite{Moser2020} developed
a graph-based MILP for DH expansion planning with nodal energy
balances, but used constant pipe efficiency factors rather than
physical $U \cdot L \cdot \Delta T$ losses. Bordin et
al.~\cite{Bordin2016} incorporated piecewise-linearized pressure
drop into a strategic DH planning MILP, but did not benchmark the
linearization against a nonlinear reference. None of these studies
isolates either topology or physics fidelity as the sole
experimental variable, nor quantifies the linearization
approximation error.

\subsection{Thermal Losses and Storage Dispatch}
\label{sec:rw_losses_storage}

Pipe thermal losses follow from steady-state or dynamic heat transfer
between fluid and soil. The steady-state linear model, in which loss
is proportional to the fluid--ground temperature
difference~\cite{Paalsson1999,Frederiksen2013}, forms the basis for
MILP-compatible loss calculations. Werner~\cite{Werner2017} reports
European DH loss ratios of 5--25\%, with modern pre-insulated
networks typically below 10\%. Talebi et al.~\cite{Talebi2016}
confirm that loss fraction increases with supply temperature and
pipe length.

Thermal energy storage (TES) provides flexibility through peak
shaving, load shifting, and renewable
integration~\cite{Vandermeulen2018,Guelpa2019review}. Christidis et
al.~\cite{Christidis2017} demonstrate that optimal TES dispatch
shifts heat production by 4--8~hours to exploit electricity price
differentials---a pattern directly dependent on accurate temporal
cost signals. In copperplate models, the only time-varying cost is
the electricity price. In network-aware models, losses introduce an
additional implicit holding cost: heat stored for later discharge
must additionally compensate for losses that are highest during cold
high-demand periods. Transport delays further modify storage dispatch
by introducing a temporal offset between injection and delivery. These
mechanisms have not been quantified in a controlled setting---a gap
this paper fills.

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Storage dispatch is the most sensitive
indicator of both topology adequacy and physics fidelity because
temporal arbitrage depends on holding costs (invisible to L1) and
delivery timing (invisible to L3). This is tested quantitatively in
\Cref{sec:results_storage}.

\subsection{COP Linearization in Heat Pump MILP Models}
\label{sec:rw_cop}

In a full optimization framework where source and sink temperatures
are decision variables, the heat pump output couples nonlinearly:
$Q_{\mathrm{hp}}(t) = \mathrm{COP}(T_{\mathrm{src}}(t),
T_{\mathrm{sink}}(t)) \cdot P_{\mathrm{hp}}^{\mathrm{el}}(t)$,
creating bilinear terms incompatible with MILP solvers. Three
linearization strategies have been proposed:
(1)~constant COP, widely used in national-scale
models~\cite{Connolly2014_EnergyPLAN,Brown2018_pypsa} but
introducing up to 30\% error for systems with variable source
temperatures~\cite{Ommen2016};
(2)~McCormick envelopes for the bilinear product, which require
temperature as an optimization variable and substantially increase
problem size~\cite{Castillo2015}; and
(3)~pre-computed COP time series, where COP is evaluated offline
from exogenous temperature data and enters the MILP as a fixed
parameter. Wirtz et al.~\cite{Wirtz2018_COP} demonstrated that
pre-computation preserves accuracy for industrial applications.
This work adopts strategy~(3) with a formal error bound
(\Cref{app:cop}).

\subsection{Controlled Experimental Design}

\subsubsection{Three Orthogonal Comparison Dimensions}

The experimental design isolates three effects through pairwise
comparisons (\Cref{fig:comparison_design}):

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Topology effect} (L1 $\to$ L2 $\to$ L3): Spatial
    resolution varies; basic physics held constant. Isolates the
    impact of network structure representation.
    \item \textbf{Physics fidelity effect} (L3 $\to$ $\Lplus$):
    Physics scope varies (adding pressure drop, temperature
    propagation, transport delay); topology and mathematical
    formulation class (MILP) held constant. Isolates the impact
    of extended thermo-hydraulic modeling.
    \item \textbf{Linearization error} ($\Lplus$ $\to$ $\Lnl$):
    Mathematical formulation varies (MILP $\to$ MIQCP); physics
    and topology held identical. Isolates the approximation error
    of piecewise linearization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Basic Physics (L1/L2/L3)}

The basic physics model is deliberately simplified and---critically
---held \emph{identical} across L2 and L3:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Steady-state thermal losses}: Per-pipe, separately
    for supply and return (\Cref{sec:model_losses}).
    \item \textbf{Fixed supply temperature}: Determined by a heating
    curve, not optimized. Eliminates temperature--flow coupling while
    preserving seasonal loss variation.
    \item \textbf{Pre-computed COP}: Time-varying parameters from
    bilinear interpolation of manufacturer data
    (\Cref{sec:model_hp}), entering as parameters to preserve MILP
    linearity.
    \item \textbf{No transport delays}: Instantaneous heat
    propagation within each hourly timestep.
    \item \textbf{No pressure optimization}: Pumping power excluded.
    \item \textbf{Dispatch-only}: All component capacities fixed.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Extended Physics ($\Lplus$/$\Lnl$)}

The extended physics model adds three phenomena to the L3 base,
using the same detailed topology:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Pressure drop}: Darcy--Weisbach friction losses
    and associated pumping power (\Cref{sec:model_pressure}).
    \item \textbf{Temperature-dependent loss propagation}:
    Downstream node temperatures depend on upstream pipe losses,
    creating a spatial temperature profile
    (\Cref{sec:model_temp_prop}).
    \item \textbf{Transport delay}: Finite heat propagation time
    through pipes based on flow velocity
    (\Cref{sec:model_delay}).
\end{itemize}

In $\Lplus$, these phenomena are linearized for MILP compatibility.
In $\Lnl$, they are modeled with native quadratic constraints
(MIQCP), serving as a fidelity benchmark.

\subsubsection{L1/L2/L3/$\Lplus$/$\Lnl$ Definitions}
\label{sec:topology_def}

\textbf{Level~1 (Copperplate):} Single bus, no spatial structure, no
pipe losses ($Q_{\mathrm{loss}}=0$ for all $t$). A single scalar heat
balance.

\textbf{Level~2 (Simplified multi-node):} Aggregated demand zones
(3--5 nodes in the primary case) connected by simplified pipe
segments with physics-based steady-state losses. The total
$U \cdot L$ product is preserved from L3, ensuring matched aggregate
annual loss energy.

\textbf{Level~3 (Detailed multi-node, basic MILP):} Full spatial
resolution from GIS data (15 nodes in the primary case). Individual
pipe parameters. Same basic loss formulation as L2. No pressure drop,
no temperature propagation, no transport delay.

\textbf{$\Lplus$ (Detailed multi-node, extended MILP):} Same
topology as L3. Adds linearized pressure drop (piecewise linear),
temperature-dependent loss propagation (linearized mixing equations),
and transport delay (fixed per pipe, computed from nominal flow
velocity). All nonlinear terms are approximated via piecewise-linear
(PWL) or Big-M formulations to preserve MILP solvability.

\textbf{$\Lnl$ (Detailed multi-node, extended MIQCP):} Same
topology and physics as $\Lplus$. Pressure drop enters as a native
quadratic constraint; temperature propagation involves bilinear
flow--temperature products handled by the MIQCP solver. Transport
delay is identical (fixed). No linearization applied. Serves as the
``ground truth'' for evaluating $\Lplus$'s linearization error.

\subsection{Basic MILP Formulation (L1/L2/L3)}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsubsection{Objective Function}

Total annual operational cost is minimized:
\begin{equation}
\min \; Z = C^{\mathrm{en}} + C^{\mathrm{fuel}}
+ C^{\mathrm{dump}} + C^{\ce{CO2}} + C^{\mathrm{dem}}
+ C^{\mathrm{pump}}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $C^{\mathrm{pump}} = 0$ for L1/L2/L3 (no pressure modeling)
and is defined in \Cref{sec:model_pressure} for $\Lplus$/$\Lnl$.

\textbf{Electricity procurement and sales:}
\begin{equation}
C^{\mathrm{en}} = \Delta t \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \left(
P_t^{\mathrm{buy}} \lambda_t^{\mathrm{buy}}
- P_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \lambda_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \right)
\label{eq:cost_energy}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_t^{\mathrm{buy}} = \lambda_t^{\mathrm{spot}}
+ \lambda^{\mathrm{fee}} + \lambda^{\mathrm{grid}}$ includes spot
price, grid fees, and levies. The selling price accounts for bid-ask
spread, floor, haircut, and export fee (full derivation in
\Cref{app:selling_price}).

\textbf{Fuel cost:}
\begin{equation}
C^{\mathrm{fuel}} = \Delta t \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{fuel}}}
\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} F_{g,t} \lambda_g^{\mathrm{fuel}}
\label{eq:cost_fuel}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Heat curtailment penalty:}
\begin{equation}
C^{\mathrm{dump}} = \Delta t \sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}}
\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dump}}
\lambda^{\mathrm{dump}}
\label{eq:cost_dump}
\end{equation}

\textbf{\ce{CO2} cost:}
\begin{equation}
C^{\ce{CO2}} = \frac{\lambda^{\ce{CO2}}}{1000} \left(
\sum_{t} E_t^{\ce{CO2},\mathrm{grid}}
+ \sum_{g,t} E_{g,t}^{\ce{CO2},\mathrm{fuel}} \right)
\label{eq:cost_co2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Demand charge} (annual peak import fee, prorated):
\begin{equation}
C^{\mathrm{dem}} = \lambda^{\mathrm{dem}} \cdot f^{\mathrm{yr}}
\cdot P^{\mathrm{peak}}
\label{eq:cost_demand}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Energy Balance Constraints}
\label{sec:model_balance}

\textbf{L1 --- Aggregated copperplate balance (no losses):}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{g} Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}}
+ \sum_{s} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}}
= Q_t^{\mathrm{dem}} + Q_t^{\mathrm{dump}}
+ \sum_{s} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}}
\qquad \forall t
\label{eq:balance_L1}
\end{equation}

\textbf{L2/L3/$\Lplus$/$\Lnl$ --- Nodal balance, producer nodes:}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_n} Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}}
+ \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_n} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}}
= Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dem}} + Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dump}}
+ \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_n} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}}
+ Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{loss}}
+ \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_n^{\mathrm{out}}} Q_{p,t}
\quad \forall n \in \mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{prod}},\, \forall t
\label{eq:balance_prod}
\end{equation}

\textbf{L2/L3/$\Lplus$/$\Lnl$ --- Nodal balance, consumer nodes:}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_n^{\mathrm{in}}} \big(Q_{p,t}
- Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,pipe}}\big)
+ \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_n} Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}}
+ \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_n} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}}
= Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dem}} + Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dump}}
+ \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_n} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}}
+ \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_n^{\mathrm{out}}} Q_{p,t}
\quad \forall n \in \mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{con}},\, \forall t
\label{eq:balance_consumer}
\end{equation}

The contrast between Eqs.~\eqref{eq:balance_L1} and
\eqref{eq:balance_prod} encodes the central experimental variable
for the topology comparison: L1's missing loss term
$Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{loss}}$ is the sole structural difference driving
the L1$\to$L2 gap.

\textbf{Global electricity balance:}
\begin{equation}
P_t^{\mathrm{buy}} + \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{CHP}}}
P_{g,t}^{\mathrm{el}}
= \sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}} P_{h,t}^{\mathrm{el}}
+ \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} P_{r,t}^{\mathrm{el}}
+ P_t^{\mathrm{pump}}
+ P_t^{\mathrm{sell}}
\qquad \forall t
\label{eq:balance_el}
\end{equation}
where $P_t^{\mathrm{pump}} = 0$ for L1/L2/L3 and is defined in
\Cref{sec:model_pressure} for $\Lplus$/$\Lnl$.

Grid import/export mutual exclusivity (Big-M):
\begin{equation}
P_t^{\mathrm{buy}} \leq M^{\mathrm{grid}} z_t, \qquad
P_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \leq M^{\mathrm{grid}} (1 - z_t)
\qquad \forall t
\label{eq:grid_bigm}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
P_t^{\mathrm{buy}} \leq \bar{P}^{\mathrm{imp}}, \qquad
P_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \leq \bar{P}^{\mathrm{exp}}
\qquad \forall t
\label{eq:grid_cap}
\end{equation}

The Big-M constant is set to
$M^{\mathrm{grid}} = \max(\bar{P}^{\mathrm{imp}},
\bar{P}^{\mathrm{exp}})$, the tightest valid bound.

Peak import tracking:
\begin{equation}
P^{\mathrm{peak}} \geq P_t^{\mathrm{buy}} \qquad \forall t
\label{eq:peak}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Thermal Loss Model --- Basic (L2/L3)}
\label{sec:model_losses}

Losses are computed separately for supply and return pipes using
steady-state conduction with \emph{fixed} (pre-computed)
temperatures:
\begin{align}
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,sup}} &= \frac{U_p L_p \big(
T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t) - T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)\big)}{10^6}
\quad [\mathrm{MW}]
\label{eq:loss_supply} \\
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,ret}} &= \frac{U_p L_p \big(
T_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mathrm{nom}} - T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)\big)}{10^6}
\quad [\mathrm{MW}]
\label{eq:loss_return}
\end{align}
where $U_p$~[\si{W/(m.K)}] is the linear heat transfer coefficient,
$L_p$~[m] the pipe length, and $T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)$ the measured
ground temperature. These losses enter the optimization as
\emph{fixed parameters} (not decision variables) because temperatures
are pre-determined by the heating curve. This is the key simplification
that makes L2/L3 purely linear. For L1,
$Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss}} = 0$ by design.

For modern pre-insulated pipes (EN~253 standard), $U_p$ typically
ranges from 0.15--0.40~\si{W/(m.K)}, varying with pipe diameter,
insulation class, and burial depth~\cite{Frederiksen2013}.

\subsubsection{Component Models}
\label{sec:model_components}

\textbf{Heat pumps.}
\label{sec:model_hp}
Each heat pump $h$ supports dual-source operation: waste heat
recovery (WRG) with time-varying COP, and a default source
(ambient/ground) with constant COP:
\begin{align}
Q_{h,t} &= Q_{h,t}^{\mathrm{wrg}} + Q_{h,t}^{\mathrm{def}}
\label{eq:hp_split} \\
P_{h,t}^{\mathrm{el}} &= \frac{Q_{h,t}^{\mathrm{wrg}}}{
\mathrm{COP}_{h,t}^{\mathrm{wrg}}}
+ \frac{Q_{h,t}^{\mathrm{def}}}{\mathrm{COP}_h^{\mathrm{def}}}
\label{eq:hp_pel}
\end{align}
$\mathrm{COP}_{h,t}^{\mathrm{wrg}}$ is pre-computed offline
(\Cref{app:cop}); the product $Q/\mathrm{COP}$ with fixed COP is
linear in the decision variable $Q$.

Capacity and minimum part-load:
\begin{equation}
\phi_h^{\mathrm{min}} \bar{Q}_h u_{h,t} \leq Q_{h,t}
\leq \bar{Q}_h u_{h,t}
\qquad \forall h,\, \forall t
\label{eq:hp_cap}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Thermal storage.} State-of-charge dynamics with
multiplicative self-discharge:
\begin{equation}
E_{s,t} = (1-\alpha_s)^{\Delta t} E_{s,t-1}
+ \eta_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}} Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}} \Delta t
- \frac{Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}} \Delta t}{\eta_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}}}
\qquad \forall s,\, \forall t
\label{eq:storage_soc}
\end{equation}
with energy capacity $E_{s,t} \leq \bar{E}_s a_{s,t}$, power
capacity $Q_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch/dis}} \leq \bar{P}_s
\delta_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch/dis}}$, and simultaneous
charge/discharge prevention
$\delta_{s,t}^{\mathrm{ch}} + \delta_{s,t}^{\mathrm{dis}}
\leq a_{s,t}$.

\textbf{Gas boiler and electrode boiler:}
\begin{align}
Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}} &= \eta_g^{\mathrm{th}} F_{g,t},
\quad Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}} \leq \bar{Q}_g
& \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{th}}
\label{eq:gen_thermal} \\
Q_{r,t} = \eta_{r,t} P_{r,t}^{\mathrm{el}},
\quad Q_{r,t} \leq \bar{Q}_r
& \forall r \in \mathcal{R}
\label{eq:p2h}
\end{align}

\textbf{CHP unit:}
$Q_{g,t}^{\mathrm{th}} = \eta_g^{\mathrm{th}} F_{g,t}$ and
$P_{g,t}^{\mathrm{el}} = \eta_g^{\mathrm{el}} F_{g,t}$ with
fixed heat-to-power ratio.

\subsubsection{Emissions Accounting}

Grid electricity and fuel emissions:
\begin{align}
E_t^{\ce{CO2},\mathrm{grid}} &= P_t^{\mathrm{buy}}
\cdot e_t^{\mathrm{grid}} \cdot \Delta t
\label{eq:em_grid} \\
E_{g,t}^{\ce{CO2},\mathrm{fuel}} &= F_{g,t}
\cdot e_g^{\mathrm{fuel}} \cdot \Delta t
\label{eq:em_fuel}
\end{align}

\subsection{Extended Physics Formulation --- $\Lplus$ (MILP)}
\label{sec:milp_extended}

$\Lplus$ augments L3 with three physical phenomena, each linearized
to preserve MILP solvability. The same detailed 15-node topology is
used; only the physics scope changes.

\subsubsection{Pressure Drop Model}
\label{sec:model_pressure}

The Darcy--Weisbach equation relates pressure drop to mass flow:
\begin{equation}
\Delta p_{p} = f_D \frac{L_p}{D_p}
\frac{8 \dot{m}_p^2}{\rho^2 \pi^2 D_p^4}
\qquad [\mathrm{Pa}]
\label{eq:darcy}
\end{equation}
where $f_D$ is the Darcy friction factor (computed from the
Colebrook--White equation at nominal Reynolds number and fixed for
each pipe), $D_p$ is the inner pipe diameter, and $\dot{m}_p$ is
the mass flow rate. The mass flow is linked to the thermal power
variable:
\begin{equation}
\dot{m}_{p,t} = \frac{Q_{p,t}}{c_p \big(
T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)
- T_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)\big)}
\label{eq:massflow}
\end{equation}

Since $T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)$ and
$T_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)$ are pre-computed parameters,
$\dot{m}_{p,t}$ is linear in $Q_{p,t}$. However, substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:massflow} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:darcy} yields a
\emph{quadratic} dependence on $Q_{p,t}$.

\textbf{MILP linearization (PWL):} The quadratic pressure drop is
approximated using a piecewise-linear function with $K$ breakpoints:
\begin{equation}
\Delta p_{p,t}^{\mathrm{PWL}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
m_k^{(p)} \cdot w_{p,t,k}
\label{eq:pressure_pwl}
\end{equation}
subject to SOS2 (Special Ordered Set of type~2) constraints on the
weights $w_{p,t,k}$ ensuring convex combination of adjacent
breakpoints. The breakpoints are spaced at
$Q_{p,k} = k \cdot \bar{Q}_p / K$ for $k = 0, \ldots, K$, with
slopes $m_k^{(p)}$ evaluated from Eq.~\eqref{eq:darcy}. With
$K = 5$ breakpoints, the worst-case PWL approximation error is
bounded by:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{\Delta p} \leq
\frac{(\bar{Q}_p / K)^2}{8} \max_{Q}
\left|\frac{d^2 (\Delta p)}{d Q^2}\right|
\label{eq:pwl_error_bound}
\end{equation}

Total network pumping power:
\begin{equation}
P_t^{\mathrm{pump}} = \frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{pump}}}
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}} \frac{\dot{m}_{p,t}
\cdot \Delta p_{p,t}}{\rho}
\label{eq:pump_power}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_{\mathrm{pump}}$ is the pump efficiency. In $\Lplus$,
the product $\dot{m}_{p,t} \cdot \Delta p_{p,t}$ is a product of
two linear-in-$Q$ terms, yielding a quartic in $Q$. This is
approximated via a second PWL layer or, equivalently, the pumping
power is directly piecewise-linearized as a function of $Q_{p,t}$
(cubic relationship):
\begin{equation}
P_{p,t}^{\mathrm{pump,PWL}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
c_k^{(p)} \cdot w_{p,t,k}
\label{eq:pump_pwl}
\end{equation}
where $c_k^{(p)}$ are pre-computed pumping power values at each
breakpoint flow rate.

\textbf{Pumping cost:}
\begin{equation}
C^{\mathrm{pump}} = \Delta t \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}}
P_t^{\mathrm{pump}} \cdot \lambda_t^{\mathrm{buy}}
\label{eq:cost_pump}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Temperature-Dependent Loss Propagation}
\label{sec:model_temp_prop}

In the basic model (L2/L3), pipe losses are computed using the
\emph{nominal} supply temperature from the heating curve,
independent of upstream losses. In physical networks, heat losses
along upstream pipes reduce the fluid temperature arriving at
downstream nodes. This creates a cascade: the actual temperature
at an intermediate node is lower than $T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}$,
reducing losses on downstream pipes relative to the basic model's
assumption.

The steady-state temperature at the outlet of pipe $p$ (connecting
node $n_1$ to node $n_2$) is:
\begin{equation}
T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}} = T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)
+ \big(T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} - T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)\big)
\cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{-U_p L_p}{\dot{m}_{p,t} c_p}\right)
\label{eq:temp_prop}
\end{equation}

This equation introduces two nonlinearities:
(1)~the exponential decay as a function of mass flow
$\dot{m}_{p,t}$, and (2)~the dependence on the upstream node
temperature $T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}}$, which is itself a decision
variable in the extended model (it depends on the mixing of incoming
pipe flows and local generation).

\textbf{MILP linearization:} For the linearized $\Lplus$
formulation, the temperature propagation is approximated in two
steps:

\emph{Step~1: Linearized temperature decay.} The exponential decay
factor $\phi_p(\dot{m}) = \exp(-U_p L_p / (\dot{m} c_p))$ is
pre-computed at $K$ mass flow breakpoints and piecewise-linearly
interpolated:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{p,t}^{\mathrm{PWL}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
\phi_p(\dot{m}_k) \cdot w_{p,t,k}
\label{eq:decay_pwl}
\end{equation}

\emph{Step~2: Linearized mixing.} The product
$T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} \cdot \phi_{p,t}^{\mathrm{PWL}}$ is a
bilinear term. In $\Lplus$, this is handled by fixing the upstream
temperature to its nominal (heating-curve) value for the
multiplication, then applying a first-order correction:
\begin{equation}
T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}} \approx T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)
+ \big(T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)
- T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)\big) \cdot \phi_{p,t}^{\mathrm{PWL}}
+ \phi_{p,t}^{\mathrm{nom}}
\cdot \big(T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}}
- T_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{nom}}(t)\big)
\label{eq:temp_prop_lin}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{p,t}^{\mathrm{nom}} = \phi_p(\dot{m}_p^{\mathrm{nom}})$
is the decay factor at nominal flow. This first-order Taylor
expansion around the nominal operating point preserves linearity in
both $T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}}$ and $\dot{m}_{p,t}$ (the latter
through the PWL weights).

The pipe loss is then updated from the propagated temperatures:
\begin{equation}
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,ext}} = \dot{m}_{p,t} c_p
\big(T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} - T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)
\label{eq:loss_extended}
\end{equation}

In $\Lplus$, this product is again linearized: since
$\dot{m}_{p,t}$ and $(T_{n_1,t} - T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}})$ are
both decision variables, the product is approximated using
McCormick envelopes or, more practically, by substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:temp_prop_lin} to express the loss as a function of
flow alone (at the linearized temperature):
\begin{equation}
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,ext}} \approx
\frac{U_p L_p}{10^6} \cdot
\bar{T}_{n_1,p,t}^{\Delta}
\label{eq:loss_ext_lin}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{T}_{n_1,p,t}^{\Delta}$ is the effective
mean temperature excess along the pipe, computed from the linearized
temperature profile.

\textbf{Nodal temperature balance ($\Lplus$):} At consumer nodes
receiving flow from multiple pipes, the supply temperature is the
flow-weighted average of incoming pipe outlet temperatures. For
the tree topology of the case study (single incoming pipe per
consumer node), this simplifies to
$T_{n,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} = T_{p_n^{\mathrm{in}},t}^{\mathrm{out}}$.

\subsubsection{Transport Delay Model}
\label{sec:model_delay}

Heat injected at node $n_1$ at time $t$ arrives at the downstream
end of pipe $p$ at time $t + \tau_p$, where the transport delay is:
\begin{equation}
\tau_p = \frac{L_p}{v_p^{\mathrm{nom}}}
= \frac{L_p \rho A_p}{\dot{m}_p^{\mathrm{nom}}}
\label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}

In principle, $\tau_p$ varies with flow rate. For the MILP
formulation, we adopt a \emph{fixed delay} computed at nominal
flow conditions, discretized to the nearest integer number of
timesteps:
\begin{equation}
k_p = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{\tau_p}{\Delta t}\right)
\label{eq:delay_discrete}
\end{equation}

The delayed energy balance at consumer node $n_2$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{delivered}} =
\big(Q_{p,t-k_p} - Q_{p,t-k_p}^{\mathrm{loss,pipe}}\big)
\qquad \forall p,\, \forall t \geq k_p + 1
\label{eq:delayed_balance}
\end{equation}

This replaces the instantaneous pipe flow term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:balance_consumer} for $\Lplus$ and $\Lnl$. For the
first $k_p$ timesteps, an initial condition
$Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{delivered}} = Q_p^{\mathrm{init}}$ (warm-start
from steady-state) is applied.

\textbf{Effect on storage dispatch:} Transport delay introduces
a temporal asymmetry: heat stored and discharged at the production
node reaches consumers $k_p$ hours later. For the case study's
longest pipe path (j$_1 \to$ j$_{15}$, \placeholder{2,125}~m at
\placeholder{1.0}~m/s), $\tau \approx \placeholder{0.6}$~h,
discretized to $k_p = 1$ timestep. This is marginal for the
primary case but becomes significant for longer networks in the
synthetic analysis (\Cref{sec:results_generalizability}).

\textbf{Note on delay discretization:} The fixed-delay
approximation introduces a discretization error of up to
$\pm \Delta t / 2$. For $\Delta t = 1$~h, this is acceptable when
$\tau_p \gg 0.5$~h. For short pipes with $\tau_p < 0.5$~h, the
delay rounds to zero, recovering the L3 instantaneous assumption.
A variable-delay formulation (where $\tau_p$ depends on the
optimized flow rate) would require additional binary variables for
delay selection---a direction left for future work.

\subsection{Quadratic Reference Formulation --- $\Lnl$ (MIQCP)}
\label{sec:miqcp}

$\Lnl$ uses the same topology, component models, and boundary
conditions as $\Lplus$, but replaces all piecewise-linear and
first-order approximations with native quadratic and bilinear
constraints. The resulting problem is a mixed-integer quadratically
constrained program (MIQCP), solvable by modern branch-and-bound
solvers (Gurobi, CPLEX) that support convex and non-convex
quadratic constraints.

\subsubsection{Pressure Drop --- Quadratic}

The Darcy--Weisbach pressure drop enters directly as a quadratic
constraint:
\begin{equation}
\Delta p_{p,t} = R_p \cdot Q_{p,t}^2
\qquad \forall p,\, \forall t
\label{eq:darcy_quad}
\end{equation}
where $R_p = f_D L_p 8 / (\rho^2 \pi^2 D_p^5 c_p^2
\Delta T_{\mathrm{nom}}^2)$ is a pre-computed pipe resistance
coefficient that absorbs the mass-flow-to-heat-flow conversion
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:massflow}. This is a convex quadratic
constraint (since $Q_{p,t} \geq 0$ in a tree network with fixed
flow direction), handled natively by Gurobi's QCP solver without
spatial branching.

Pumping power:
\begin{equation}
P_{p,t}^{\mathrm{pump}} = \frac{Q_{p,t}
\cdot \Delta p_{p,t}}{\eta_{\mathrm{pump}} \rho
c_p \Delta T_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)}
= \frac{R_p}{\eta_{\mathrm{pump}} \rho c_p
\Delta T_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)} \cdot Q_{p,t}^3
\label{eq:pump_power_quad}
\end{equation}

The cubic term $Q_{p,t}^3$ is modeled by introducing an auxiliary
variable $W_{p,t} = Q_{p,t}^2$ (quadratic constraint) and then
the bilinear product $P_{p,t}^{\mathrm{pump}} \propto Q_{p,t}
\cdot W_{p,t}$:
\begin{align}
W_{p,t} &= Q_{p,t}^2
\label{eq:aux_quad} \\
P_{p,t}^{\mathrm{pump}} &= \frac{R_p}{\eta_{\mathrm{pump}} \rho
c_p \Delta T_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)} \cdot Q_{p,t} \cdot W_{p,t}
\label{eq:pump_bilinear}
\end{align}

\textbf{Note:} The bilinear product in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pump_bilinear}
is non-convex. Gurobi handles this via spatial branching
(non-convex MIQCP), which may increase solve time. For the case
study scale (15~nodes, 14~pipes, 8,760~hours), this remains
tractable (\Cref{sec:results_computation}).

\subsubsection{Temperature Propagation --- Bilinear}

The temperature propagation equation is modeled without
linearization:
\begin{equation}
T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}} = T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)
+ \big(T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} - T_{\mathrm{ground}}(t)\big)
\cdot \phi_{p,t}
\label{eq:temp_prop_quad}
\end{equation}

The decay factor is pre-computed as a piecewise-linear function of
flow (same PWL as $\Lplus$---this is a practical concession; the
exponential itself cannot be represented in MIQCP). The key
difference from $\Lplus$ is that the product
$T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} \cdot \phi_{p,t}$ is retained as a
\emph{bilinear} term, not linearized via Taylor expansion:
\begin{equation}
\Theta_{p,t} = T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} \cdot \phi_{p,t}
\label{eq:bilinear_temp}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta_{p,t}$ is an auxiliary variable with the bilinear
constraint handled natively by the MIQCP solver.

The pipe loss becomes:
\begin{equation}
Q_{p,t}^{\mathrm{loss,ext}} = \dot{m}_{p,t} c_p
\big(T_{n_1,t}^{\mathrm{sup}} - T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)
\label{eq:loss_ext_quad}
\end{equation}
which again involves a bilinear product $\dot{m}_{p,t} \cdot
(T_{n_1,t} - T_{p,t}^{\mathrm{out}})$, modeled natively.

\subsubsection{Transport Delay --- Identical}

The transport delay model is identical to $\Lplus$
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:delay}--\eqref{eq:delayed_balance}), using fixed
delays at nominal flow. This is the only approximation shared
between $\Lplus$ and $\Lnl$; a fully variable delay would require
integer-indexed temporal coupling beyond MIQCP capability.

\subsection{Demand Heterogeneity Index}
\label{sec:model_hi}

The demand heterogeneity index HI quantifies spatial non-uniformity
of demand:
\begin{equation}
d_n = \frac{\sum_t Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dem}}}{\sum_n \sum_t
Q_{n,t}^{\mathrm{dem}}}, \qquad
\mathrm{HI} = \frac{N}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^{N}
\left(d_n - \frac{1}{N}\right)^2
\label{eq:hi}
\end{equation}
HI~$=0$ indicates uniform demand; HI~$=1$ indicates complete
concentration at a single node.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:model_impl}

The model is implemented in Python~3.10 using
Pyomo~\cite{pyomo}. All five variants (L1/L2/L3/$\Lplus$/$\Lnl$)
are instantiated from a single parameterized codebase via YAML
configuration files. Solver: Gurobi~10.0 for all variants, with
settings:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{L1/L2/L3/$\Lplus$:} MILP mode,
    MIP gap~$\leq$~0.01\%.
    \item \textbf{$\Lnl$:} Non-convex MIQCP mode
    (\texttt{NonConvex=2}), MIP gap~$\leq$~0.1\%
    (relaxed to accommodate spatial branching overhead).
\end{itemize}
Identical random seeds across all runs. Cost differences
$\geq$0.1\% between L1/L2/L3 and $\geq$0.2\% between
$\Lplus$/$\Lnl$ are thus attributable to model structure, not
solver tolerance.

The $\Lnl$ solve uses Gurobi's spatial branching algorithm for
non-convex quadratic constraints. For the case study scale
(15~nodes, $\approx$8,760 timesteps), the solver terminates within
the gap tolerance for all instances (\Cref{tab:losses_computation}).
For larger synthetic networks (30 nodes), the $\Lnl$ gap tolerance
is relaxed to 0.5\% to ensure termination within 24~hours.

Single bus. No pipe losses. Total demand equals sum of all junction

Same 15-node topology as L3. Adds:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item PWL pressure drop ($K=5$ segments per pipe)
    \item Linearized temperature propagation
    (Taylor expansion around nominal)
    \item Fixed transport delay ($k_p$ per pipe from
    \Cref{tab:transport_delays})
    \item Pumping power in objective and electricity balance
\end{itemize}

additional variables: $\approx |\mathcal{P}| \times |\mathcal{T}|

Same topology and physics as $\Lplus$. Replaces PWL pressure drop

Taylor-linearized temperature propagation with bilinear constraints
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bilinear_temp}. The exponential decay factor remains
PWL (shared with $\Lplus$). Transport delay is identical (fixed).

Additional quadratic constraints per pipe per timestep: 1~(pressure
drop) + 1~(bilinear temperature mixing) = $2 \times 14 \times 8760
\approx 245{,}000$ quadratic constraints.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Effect Hierarchy and Practical Implications}
\label{sec:disc_hierarchy}

The results establish a clear hierarchy of modeling effects:
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{\text{L1} \to \text{L2}}_{\text{largest}}
\; > \;
\underbrace{\text{L3} \to \Lplus}_{\text{intermediate}}
\; \geq \;
\underbrace{\text{L2} \to \text{L3}}_{\text{context-dep.}}
\; \gg \;
\underbrace{\Lplus \to \Lnl}_{\text{negligible}}
\end{equation*}

This hierarchy has three practical implications:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Physics inclusion dominates topology refinement.}
    Adding thermal losses (L1$\to$L2) consistently produces larger
    cost corrections than refining spatial resolution (L2$\to$L3).
    The implication is clear: a 3-zone model with losses is more
    accurate than a 15-node model without.

    \item \textbf{Extended physics matter for long networks.} The
    L3$\to\Lplus$ gap is negligible for compact networks
    ($<$2~km) but significant for networks where cumulative
    transport delay exceeds $\approx$1~h or pumping costs exceed
    $\approx$2\% of thermal losses. This provides a quantitative
    threshold for practitioners.

    \item \textbf{Linearization is adequate for planning.} The
    $\Lplus\to\Lnl$ gap confirms that piecewise-linear
    approximations with $K \geq 5$ segments introduce negligible
    error for DH planning. Practitioners can use MILP solvers
    with confidence; the MIQCP formulation serves as a validation
    benchmark rather than a production tool.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Storage Dispatch as Universal Diagnostic}
\label{sec:disc_storage_diagnostic}

Across all three comparison dimensions, storage dispatch metrics
(charge/discharge timing, SOC profile, cycling frequency) prove to
be the most sensitive indicators of model adequacy:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item L1$\to$L2: Charge timing shifts
    \placeholder{X}~h (loss-driven holding cost).
    \item L3$\to\Lplus$: Discharge timing shifts
    \placeholder{X.X}~h (transport delay); peak shaving
    improves (pumping cost incentive).
    \item $\Lplus\to\Lnl$: Negligible shift
    ($<$\placeholder{0.X}~h), confirming linearization
    adequacy.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical recommendation:} Before reporting cost or
emission results from any topology/physics level, practitioners
should compare storage dispatch patterns against the next-higher
fidelity level. If charge/discharge timing differs by more than
1~h, the lower-fidelity model is likely inadequate for the
application.

\subsection{\ce{CO2} Policy Implications}
\label{sec:disc_co2}

Simplified models underestimate \ce{CO2} emissions through
compounding channels:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item L1: Heat pump over-dispatch + missed boiler activations
    at peak-loss hours.
    \item L3 (vs.\ $\Lplus$): Missed pumping electricity demand.
    \item Temporal correlation: Loss/pumping-driven additional
    electricity demand occurs at specific hours whose grid
    emission factors may systematically differ from hours at
    which simplified models schedule operations.
\end{itemize}

For industrial networks subject to carbon reporting obligations,
L1-based emission accounting introduces a systematic
underestimation of \placeholder{X--Y}~t\ce{CO2}/year.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Fixed transport delay}: Variable delay (depending
    on optimized flow) would require additional binaries for delay
    selection, left for future work.
    \item \textbf{PWL exponential decay}: Both $\Lplus$ and $\Lnl$
    approximate the exponential temperature decay as PWL; a fully
    nonlinear formulation (MINLP) would require a different solver.
    \item \textbf{Steady-state hydraulics}: No water hammer or
    transient pressure effects.
    \item \textbf{Hourly resolution}: Sub-hourly peaks and
    transport delays $<$30~min not captured.
    \item \textbf{Single validation case}: Generalization relies on
    synthetic parametric analysis.
    \item \textbf{Tree topology only}: Ring networks require flow
    direction binaries (deferred).
    \item \textbf{Dispatch-only}: Investment sizing effects not
    examined.
    \item \textbf{Single year of data}: Interannual variability
    partially addressed via sensitivity analysis.
    \item \textbf{Non-convex MIQCP}: Gurobi's spatial branching
    guarantees global optimality but solve times scale
    unfavorably for larger instances.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents the first controlled comparison that
systematically isolates topology abstraction, physics fidelity, and
linearization error in district heating dispatch optimization. Five
model variants (L1, L2, L3, $\Lplus$, $\Lnl$) are embedded in a
unified framework validated against an industrial heat network.

\textbf{Principal findings:}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Topology effect (RQ1):} The L1$\to$L2 transition
    dominates: cost underestimation of \placeholder{X}\%, \ce{CO2}
    underestimation of \placeholder{X}\%. The mechanism is
    loss-driven generation underestimation.

    \item \textbf{Physics fidelity effect (RQ2):} The L3$\to\Lplus$
    transition adds \placeholder{X--Y}\% cost through pumping and
    transport delay effects. Significant for networks with pipe
    length $>$5~km or transport delay $>$1~h.

    \item \textbf{Linearization error (RQ3):} The $\Lplus\to\Lnl$
    gap is \placeholder{$<$X}\%, validating MILP-based
    approximations of thermo-hydraulic physics for planning.

    \item \textbf{Generalizability (RQ4):} The L1$\to$L2 gap
    scales with pipe length; the L2$\to$L3 gap with demand
    heterogeneity; the L3$\to\Lplus$ gap with pipe length and
    cumulative transport delay.

    \item \textbf{Storage dispatch diagnostic:} Charge/discharge
    timing is the most sensitive indicator across all comparison
    dimensions, shifted by up to \placeholder{X}~h between L1
    and $\Lnl$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Recommendations:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item L2 as minimum fidelity for any system with storage or
    pipe length $>$1~km.
    \item L3 when HI~$>$~0.4 or storage ratio~$>$~6~h with
    heterogeneous demand.
    \item $\Lplus$ when pipe length $>$5~km, transport delay
    $>$1~h, or pumping cost $>$2\% of thermal losses.
    \item $\Lnl$ as a one-time validation benchmark, not for
    production use.
    \item Validate storage dispatch patterns between fidelity
    levels before reporting cost or emission results.
    \item Use a staged L1$\to$L2$\to$L3$\to\Lplus$ workflow,
    upgrading fidelity as project detail increases.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Future work:} Ring topologies; variable transport delay
optimization; full MINLP with exponential temperature decay;
stochastic variants; investment--dispatch co-optimization;
sub-hourly resolution; multi-year variability; extension to
5th-generation (bidirectional) networks.

\section{PWL Approximation Error Bounds}
\label{app:pwl}

\subsection{Pressure Drop PWL}

For $\Delta p = R Q^2$ with $K$ equal segments on $[0, \bar{Q}]$:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{\Delta p}^{\max} = \frac{R \bar{Q}^2}{4 K^2}
\end{equation}
With $K=5$: $\varepsilon_{\Delta p}^{\max} = R \bar{Q}^2 / 100$,
i.e., 1\% of peak pressure drop.

\subsection{Temperature Decay PWL}

For $\phi(\dot{m}) = \exp(-c/\dot{m})$ with $K$ segments:
the approximation error depends on the curvature
$|d^2\phi/d\dot{m}^2|$, which is highest at low flow. With $K=5$
and the minimum flow set to 10\% of nominal, the worst-case error
is bounded at \placeholder{X}\% of $\phi$.

\subsection{Taylor Linearization Error ($\Lplus$ only)}

The first-order Taylor expansion of
$T^{\mathrm{out}} \approx T^{\mathrm{out,nom}}
+ \phi^{\mathrm{nom}} \cdot (T^{\mathrm{in}} - T^{\mathrm{in,nom}})$
neglects the second-order term
$\frac{1}{2} (T^{\mathrm{in}} - T^{\mathrm{in,nom}})^2
\cdot d\phi/dT^{\mathrm{in}}|_{\mathrm{nom}}$.
Since $\phi$ does not depend on $T^{\mathrm{in}}$ (only on
$\dot{m}$), the Taylor error arises solely from the bilinear
$T \cdot \phi$ product. For typical deviations
$|T - T^{\mathrm{nom}}| < \placeholder{X}$~K and
$|\phi - \phi^{\mathrm{nom}}| < \placeholder{X.XX}$, the neglected
cross-term is bounded at
$\Delta T \cdot \Delta \phi < \placeholder{X.X}$~K, contributing
$<$\placeholder{X}\% to pipe losses.
