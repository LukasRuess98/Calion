\subsection{Cost accounting: economic cost versus the solver objective}
\label{subsec:costacct}
Every bias and regret figure in this paper is reported on the economic operating cost, not on
the raw solver objective, and the two differ systematically. On Memmingen the objective
exceeds the economic cost by roughly forty percent, varying only slightly across levels
(Table~\ref{tab:objdecomp}, reported in Section~\ref{subsec:validation}). The gap is
near-constant in absolute euros because it depends on the generation mix and the storage
duty, neither of which moves much between levels. Because it is large enough to change the
apparent size of every comparison, this section states what it is and why the economic cost
is the right quantity. 

Two mechanisms account for it, and neither is a distortion of the dispatch. First, carbon enters
the objective on a gross basis (every tonne from fuel combustion and grid import priced at the
carbon price), whereas the reported cost applies the standard cogeneration self-use allocation,
crediting back the emissions of combined-heat-and-power electricity that is exported rather than
self-consumed. This is the avoided-emissions convention conventional in district-heating studies.
On this network the credit is of the order of several tens of thousands of euros per year (see
Table~\ref{tab:objdecomp}). Second, the objective carries a thermal-storage cycling penalty of
2\,EUR per MWh of throughput, which shapes the solution away from physically implausible rapid
cycling but corresponds to no operator cash flow. The cycling penalty is similarly of the order of
tens of thousands of euros per year (Table~\ref{tab:objdecomp}). Together these reproduce the
residual to within about one percent of the objective.

The economic cost is reported because it is what an operator pays and the only quantity a dispatch
decision changes. Both accounting terms are near-constant across fidelity levels (they depend on
the generation mix and the storage duty, which barely move), so they inflate every level's
absolute cost by roughly the same amount and therefore dilute the percentage differences between
levels rather than distorting them. The copperplate's estimation bias differs by several
percentage points between the two bases (detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:decomp}), but the finding
is the same: measured against a base that does or does not include terms no operator sees. In
every bias and regret difference the accounting terms cancel, so the comparisons themselves are
unaffected by the choice.

The carbon convention is itself a modelling decision, stated explicitly: exported cogeneration
electricity is credited with the emissions it displaces at the grid margin. The alternative gross
convention shifts magnitudes by two to three percentage points and changes neither the sign of
any bias or regret figure nor the loss-dominance result, because the credit is near-identical at
every level. The per-level split is released as \texttt{objective\_decomposition.csv} so that either basis can be reconstructed.
