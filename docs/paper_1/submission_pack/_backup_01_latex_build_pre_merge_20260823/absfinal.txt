Operational dispatch models for district-heating networks routinely simplify the pipe network, yet how much these simplifications distort the resulting decisions is rarely measured. Existing comparisons vary spatial resolution and thermal-loss representation together, so the two cannot be separated, and they compare objective values rather than the cost of the schedules produced. We separate two quantities: estimation bias, the difference in optimised cost between formulations, and decision regret, the extra cost when a simpler model's schedule is run under a common high-fidelity physics reference. Both are measured on a fidelity ladder that adds one phenomenon at a time, from a single-node "copperplate" through node-resolved losses, pipe pressure and station hydraulics, plus a copperplate given the aggregate loss it cannot compute, which isolates loss from topology exactly. We study one real industrial network and 135 synthetic networks. Loss visibility, not spatial layout, is decisive: the copperplate-to-detailed cost gap is 95.8 % a loss effect and only 4.7 % a topology effect. On operating cost a copperplate scores -15.1 % against the detailed model, but +46.1 % once its schedule is run: a biased estimator and an unreliable controller at once. Adding pressure, temperature propagation, transport delay and station hydraulics changes cost by under 1 % and decisions by almost nothing; a lumped loss correction hides the gap on one network but does not transfer. The practical conclusion is to resolve the network so losses become visible before refining the physics; the findings hold for radial, centrally-supplied networks dispatched at fixed capacity.
