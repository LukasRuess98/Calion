%% Numbers \result{}-filled from the HARDENED CSVs (decomposition_live.csv,

%% Do NOT write "dominates"; do NOT write "accuracy" except of decisions.
%% ---- DRAFTED (author: polish voice only) ----
District-heating dispatch-optimisation models are simplified in ways whose decision
cost is rarely quantified, and prior fidelity comparisons both vary network and loss
representation together (so neither can be attributed) and judge fidelity by
comparing objective values across formulations, which measures the models rather than
the decisions they produce. We separate two quantities: estimation bias (the objective
difference between formulations) and decision regret (the cost of executing a model's
dispatch schedule, obtained by re-simulating it under a common high-fidelity forward
model with native-exponential losses, computed-friction hydraulics and the consumer
differential-pressure requirement, and no piecewise linearisation). That forward model is
not treated as ground truth; it is a common benchmark against which the sensitivity of
dispatch \emph{decisions} to model fidelity is measured. We evaluate both on
a one-phenomenon-per-step fidelity ladder (from a copperplate through node-resolved
losses, trunk pressure and station-resolved hydraulics) augmented by a copperplate
supplied with the aggregate loss it cannot itself compute, a control that yields an
exact loss/topology decomposition, on one real network (Memmingen) and a balanced
135-network synthetic factorial. Judged by decisions rather than by objective values, a
copperplate is a biased estimator \emph{and} an incompetent controller at once: its
schedule looks \textbf{-15.1}\,\% cheaper on operating cost yet costs
\textbf{+46.1}\,\% more to execute, the two carrying opposite signs, whereas
loss-aware levels are physically deliverable. What separates them is loss visibility, not
spatial topology. The copperplate-to-baseline cost gap decomposes to machine precision into
a loss main effect of \textbf{95.8}\,\% and a spatial-topology main effect of only
\textbf{4.7}\,\%, and across the synthetic factorial loss explains a median
\textbf{100.0}\,\% of the gap, topology staying within
$\pm$0.6\,\% on every network of 5\,km trunk length or more and never exceeding 2.4\,\% even
on the shortest, where the entire gap is below 6\,\% of cost. A lumped loss adder can hide
this on any one network but does not transfer across scenarios. Resolving the demand side to all 174
individual transmission stations and their service laterals, on real component data (pump
datasheets, network plans) cross-checked against a nonlinear pipe-flow solver, changes
dispatch cost by less than 1\,\% and decisions by essentially zero, a null at maximal
fidelity that locates the threshold below station-level hydraulics. Whether spatial
resolution matters more under distributed than central generation is posed as a controlled
question, resolved as null for central generation. The results are conditional on fixed
capacities, a fixed heating curve and radial networks; joint dispatch-and-sizing is left
to a companion study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\new{\input{introduction_opening_v2}}

%% <<DRAFTED:INTRO-FRAMING>> DRAFTED (author: polish voice) -- two framing moves.
Two features of the existing fidelity literature limit what its comparisons can
conclude. First, the comparisons are confounded: moving from a copperplate or a
zone-aggregated model to a node-resolved one changes the spatial representation of the
network and the representation of thermal losses \emph{together}, so a resulting cost
difference cannot be attributed to either: the very objection that a coarse model
``misses losses'' is indistinguishable from ``misses topology''. Second, and more
fundamentally, fidelity is judged by the wrong quantity. Studies report how far the
\emph{objective value} of a simplified formulation departs from a detailed one, which
measures the models against each other; but a planning or scheduling model is used to
\emph{make a decision}, and what matters is the cost of having decided with the simpler
model and then operated the network. A formulation can misstate the objective yet
produce a schedule that executes well, or state the objective plausibly yet schedule
poorly; objective-difference comparisons cannot tell these apart. This paper addresses
both: a control condition that separates loss from topology, and a decision-regret
metric that separates estimation error from decision quality.

The five formulations compared here are established, and comparative fidelity studies
already exist: Wirtz et al.\ varied MILP variants of a multi-energy system, Larsen and
Falay studied network aggregation, and Kotzur et al.\ reviewed complexity management. Our
own earlier submission was, in this sense, a comparative application. The contribution is
not the formulations but the experimental structure and the quantity evaluated, and it
rests on two pillars. The first is a decision-oriented evaluation of fidelity. We re-cost
each model's dispatch schedule under a common high-fidelity forward model (decision regret)
and test whether the schedule is physically deliverable, and we pair this with a control
condition (a copperplate supplied with the aggregate loss it cannot itself compute)
that turns the comparison into an exact loss/topology decomposition. Together these show
that loss visibility, not spatial routing, sets the fidelity requirement; that a model can
be a biased estimator and an incompetent controller at once, its bias and regret carrying
opposite signs; and that a per-network loss adder which makes a copperplate appear
sufficient does not transfer across networks or operating regimes, so the resolved model is
the transferable one. We position the regret idea honestly as the value of the stochastic
solution~\citep{BirgeLouveaux2011}, transposed from the uncertainty axis to the resolution
axis, and claim the transfer and the empirical finding, not the concept. The second pillar establishes where the fidelity
threshold lies by pushing physical detail to its limit: resolving the demand side to the 174
individual transmission stations and their service laterals, on real component data
cross-checked against a nonlinear pipe-flow solver, changes dispatch cost by under one
percent and decisions by essentially zero: a null at maximal fidelity, and the direct
answer to the hydraulic-validation concern. Two further findings are reported as secondary:
that the spatial-routing effect is null under central generation and, for distributed
generation, is left as a controlled open question; and that an a-priori estimator of the
bias from observable network properties predicts held-out synthetic networks without
refitting. The paper is organised around four research questions (the loss/topology
decomposition, decision regret and deliverability, the transferability of a lumped-loss
correction, and the location of the hydraulic threshold), with the two secondary questions
addressed alongside.

\subsection{Experimental design: fidelity ladder and decomposition controls}

We compare a family of formulations that differ from one another in exactly one modelled
phenomenon at a time, so that each pairwise contrast isolates a single effect and no
comparison is confounded. The family has two parts. Four \emph{decomposition controls} form a
$2\times2$ factorial of spatial topology against thermal loss: a copperplate with no loss
($\CP$), the copperplate supplied with the aggregate loss ($\CPL$), the node-resolved network
with no loss ($\NDz$), and the node-resolved network with trunk loss ($\Lone$), which is also
the comparison baseline. A monotone \emph{fidelity ladder} then adds, one at a time, on the
same node-resolved network: temperature propagation ($\Ltwo$), trunk pressure and pumping
($\Lthree$), station resolution with service laterals ($\Lfour$), a dynamic flow-dependent
station pressure requirement ($\Lfive$), and transport delay ($\Lsix$), with the fully
nonlinear formulation ($\NLref$) as an unsolved reference. Table~S1 (Supplementary Material) states
what each arrow isolates. Two design principles hold throughout. First, exactly one phenomenon
changes per step, so any cost difference is attributable. Second, the 174 transmission stations
are present at every level; only their spatial aggregation changes, from all lumped at one
point through node and zone aggregation to individually resolved, so the taxonomy is consistent
across the whole family. The four controls satisfy an exact additive identity,
$\text{cost}(\Lone)-\text{cost}(\CP)=\text{loss}+\text{topology}+\text{interaction}$, where the
loss main effect is $\text{cost}(\CPL)-\text{cost}(\CP)$, the topology main effect is
$\text{cost}(\NDz)-\text{cost}(\CP)$, and the interaction is the residual; it is an identity,
not an approximation, and closes to machine precision when the four models are solved to a
0.01\,\% optimality gap.
%% <<DRAFTED:REF-DEF>> ---- DRAFTED ----
Two reference roles must be kept distinct. The baseline $\Lone$ is the detailed
mixed-integer model a practitioner would actually build; every estimation-bias percentage is
normalised to it. The \emph{physics} reference, against which regret and physical feasibility
are judged, is the forward evaluator of Section~\ref{subsec:evaluator}, \emph{not} any solved
level: even the finest solved level linearises the temperature-decay factor, so using it as
ground truth would carry the same linearisation into the reference. Model-to-model differences
are therefore estimation bias; only the forward evaluation speaks to decision quality.

\subsection{Copperplate with aggregate losses}\label{subsec:lumped}
%% <<DRAFTED:LUMPED-LOSS>> (author: polish + insert adder eqn). T0P1c dropped per scope.
%% ---- DRAFTED ----
The control condition $\CPL$ is the copperplate supplied with the aggregate network loss it
cannot itself compute, so that the loss/topology decomposition is exact. The loss enters
exogenously, by inflating the single-node demand series; the formulation stays a linear
program with the same variable count as the copperplate, and no network is added. We use two
calibrations of the adder. The first is a constant adder $L_a = E_\text{loss}/8760$, the
annual network loss spread evenly over the year; the second is a heating-curve adder
$L_b(t)=\sum_p U_p\,\ell_p\,(T_\text{sup}(t)-T_\text{g})+\sum_p U^r_p\,\ell_p\,(T_\text{ret}(t)-T_\text{g})$
that tracks the seasonal supply and return temperatures, summed over pipes $p$ with
length $\ell_p$ and supply/return heat-loss coefficients $U_p,U^r_p$. Both are frozen before
the copperplate is solved, so the copperplate never sees the reference model's dispatch, only
its aggregate loss; the two calibrations bracket the effect of the temporal loss profile and,
as reported below, give the same bias and regret to within a few tenths of a percentage point.
The adder is deliberately the network's \emph{own} realised loss: this is what makes $\CPL$ a
best case for the lumped-loss shortcut, and the finding that even this best case does not
transfer across networks (Section~\ref{subsec:generalis}) is therefore conservative.

\subsection{Forward evaluator and decision regret}
\label{subsec:evaluator}
%% <<DRAFTED:EVALUATOR>> THE NEW METHOD SECTION. (author: polish + insert equations)
%% ---- DRAFTED ----
The evaluator is a forward simulator, not an optimisation model: it takes a \emph{fixed}
dispatch schedule (the generation, storage and temperature trajectory a level produced)
and computes the true cost of executing it under high-fidelity physics, reporting any physical
constraint the schedule violates. Because it never optimises it has no tractability limit and
contains no piecewise linearisation anywhere. Heat transport along each pipe uses the native
exponential temperature decay $T_\text{out}=T_\text{g}+(T_\text{in}-T_\text{g})\exp(-U L/\dot m
c_p)$ on both the supply and the return circuit; hydraulics use Darcy--Weisbach with a
computed (Swamee--Jain) friction factor on supply and return, plus the differential-pressure
requirement at the critical consumer's substation. This is strictly a higher-fidelity
reference than the finest solved level, whose temperature-decay factor is still piecewise
linear, the caveat that made a solved level an unsound ``ground truth'' in the original
submission. Cost is accounted with the model's own conventions (energy purchase less sales,
fuel, CO$_2$, dump and demand charge), so that a schedule executed under the physics it was
optimised for reproduces its own economic cost to within the pump and residual-loss terms
the physics adds; regret then measures physics, not an accounting mismatch. Schedules from the
copperplate and zone levels, which carry no node-resolved dispatch, are disaggregated to the
network by the per-node demand share (with an equal-share alternative reported as a
robustness check); this rule is part of the method and stated as such. On this common
evaluation we define, for each level $\ell$ against the comparison baseline, the estimation
\emph{bias} as the difference in optimised economic cost and the decision \emph{regret} as the
difference in the forward-evaluated cost of the two schedules, together with the hours and
energy of any velocity, differential-pressure or unmet-demand \emph{violation}. The evaluator
is itself validated: it reproduces the baseline schedule's economic cost as a self-consistency
check, its forward losses match the model's exported network loss to about two percent (the
exponential lying slightly below the linear approximation, as expected), and its trunk
pressures agree with an independent nonlinear pipe-flow solver to better than $0.007$\,bar.

\subsection{Extended thermo-hydraulic formulation}\label{sec:extended}
\new{\input{extended_physics_v2}}
%% <<DRAFTED:HYDRAULIC-DETAIL>> Answers R2.4. (author: polish + insert DW eqns)
%% ---- DRAFTED ----
The extended formulation resolves both the supply and the return circuit. Trunk pressure drop
(from $\Lthree$) follows Darcy--Weisbach, linearised as a piecewise function of flow, and the
associated pumping power enters the objective at the pump efficiency taken from the installed
units' datasheets (Wilo IL-E). Every consumer additionally requires a differential pressure
across its substation, set to the operator's specified $0.6$\,bar; this is the term the
original submission omitted. The resulting pumping-energy share is small and, rather than being
calibrated away, is reconciled physically by the compact network and its heavily oversized pumps;
the magnitudes, and the agreement with an independent nonlinear pipe-flow solver, are reported
with the results in Section~\ref{subsec:hydraulics}.

\subsubsection{Station-resolved hydraulics (L4, L5): the test that locates the threshold}

The station levels are evaluated with the forward model rather than solved as additional
mixed-integer dispatch levels, and this is the rigorous choice, not a compromise, for four
reasons. First, the forward module uses the native nonlinear station pressure relation,
validated against real component data and the nonlinear solver (exactly the validated
nonlinear reference), whereas a mixed-integer level would re-linearise
that relation and reintroduce the very linearisation the reviewers criticised. Second,
re-costing the baseline schedule under the full station physics \emph{upper-bounds} the regret:
finding under one percent of extra cost with no feasibility violation establishes that no schedule
could be more than one percent better, which is a bound on the decision change, not a
gap-limited point estimate. Third, it is consistent with the paper's position that the physics
reference is the forward evaluator and not further piecewise-linear levels. Fourth, station
pumping is load-following (it tracks flow and hence demand), so the optimiser has no lever to
re-optimise around it, and bias, regret and the forward adder coincide by construction.

Temperature propagation ($\Ltwo$) is handled the same way, and for a related reason. Its physics
(that, given the plant supply temperature, downstream nodes are cooler because heat decays
along the pipes) is bilinear (temperature times mass flow) and therefore non-convex; making the
node temperatures free mixed-integer variables is not only intractable but \emph{degenerate},
because the optimiser then also gains the freedom to lower the temperature level itself, and
absent a hydraulic penalty it drives every node to a uniform floor rather than reproducing the
physical decay. That is temperature \emph{optimisation}, a different question we treat separately
below (Section~\ref{subsec:tsup}), not the propagation physics. We therefore isolate propagation
with the validated nonlinear forward reference, which holds the supply temperature at the
heating-curve value the schedule prescribes and propagates the native exponential decay
downstream: the difference between this and the fixed-temperature baseline is the
temperature-propagation effect, reported in Section~\ref{subsec:linearisation} and isolated by
the exact decomposition. Propagation reduces total network loss by about two percent (a few
tenths of a percent of cost), so, like the station hydraulics, it lies below the
decision-relevance threshold.

Transport delay ($\Lsix$) is a linear time shift of delivered heat and is isolated as its own
level. The fully nonlinear reference (bilinear temperature propagation and quadratic
pressure together) is not solved: the global mixed-integer quadratically-constrained program
is intractable (no incumbent within a day). Its distance from the linearised levels is instead
bounded by the exact decomposition of the linearisation error and by the forward evaluator,
which bounds it rigorously without asserting an unattainable solve.

Validation resolution bounds what fidelity can be claimed, and the Memmingen metering makes the
point concretely. Its consumer sensors are billing instruments downstream of mixing valves, so
they read several degrees below the primary junction temperature; intermediate node temperatures
are therefore not directly testable, and even the source-to-far-end corridor match is
flow-sensitive under billing-grade metering. The defensible thermal validation is therefore the
\emph{annual network loss} (matched to about one percent), the quantity the conclusions depend
on, rather than the point temperature field. The station hydraulics are validated separately against real component data and an
independent nonlinear solver. The general principle is that a model resolved finer than its
metering cannot have that extra detail verified: its additional resolution is asserted, not
tested. This has a practical
consequence and a converse. The practical consequence is that, for an operator metering at the
substation or zone, the node or zone thermal level is the finest \emph{defensible} level, not
merely the cheapest. The converse, established in the results, is that resolving hydraulics all
the way to the individual substation changes decisions by essentially nothing, so the
fidelity requirement sits below the level at which extra detail could even be validated.

The original submission calibrated the pipe losses in two ways that need reconciling. A global
boundary-condition match scaled all heat-transfer coefficients by a single factor, while a
branch-sequential fit raised the \emph{terminal} pipes' coefficients by up to a factor of
several to make the trunk-only loss match the measured total. The second is what a reviewer
rightly questioned: multiplying nominal $U$-values by such a factor cannot be justified as
insulation ageing, and what it was really absorbing was the \emph{last-mile} loss (the service
laterals and substations that a fifteen-node trunk model does not represent), forced onto the
trunk pipes because there was nowhere else for it to go. We remove the indefensible multiplier
and re-calibrate under a constraint that keeps trunk coefficients in a defensible range, moving
the residual loss into an explicit service-lateral term that enters only at the station-resolved
level (L4). The node-resolved levels then honestly \emph{undercount} the true loss (they cannot
see the last mile), and the total matches the measurement only once the laterals are added; this
is itself a result (Section~\ref{subsec:linearisation}). Crucially, this re-calibration leaves
the decomposition unchanged (loss dominance is not an artefact of the multiplier), and we report
both in-sample and out-of-sample metrics separately, since the original submission reported an
in-sample fit as validation; the flow comparison is given as a mean-absolute-percentage error
decomposed by load band, so that the fit at part load, where most hours sit, is visible
rather than hidden in an annual aggregate.

\subsection{Decision regret and physical feasibility}
%% <<DRAFTED:REGRET>> DRAFT filled from HARDENED regret_decomp.csv / regret_decomp_pricing.csv
%% (ref = L1, <=0.01% gap). TODO polish: add decision-divergence metrics (HP hours,

Estimation bias and decision regret diverge sharply, and for the copperplate they
carry \emph{opposite signs}. Its schedule appears \textbf{-15.1}\,\% cheaper
than the baseline (bias), yet re-costing that loss-ignoring schedule under the common
forward physics makes it \textbf{+46.1}\,\% more expensive to execute
(regret): a copperplate is a biased estimator \emph{and} an incompetent controller,
because it under-generates for the network losses it never saw. The asymmetry
($|\text{regret}|>|\text{bias}|$) is mechanistic: the loss the copperplate omits must
be covered under execution by topping up at the marginal or peak unit, at the winter
hours it never planned for, rather than by optimal pre-planning. The schedules show this directly: the copperplate runs the heat pump 2\,063 hours against the baseline's 2\,309 and gives it 77.9\,\% of production against 87.8\,\%: 246 fewer operating hours and ten percentage points less share, the 46\,\% regret being that substitution priced. Supplying the same
copperplate the aggregate loss it cannot compute (\CPL) collapses both bias and regret
to $\approx\textbf{-0.54}\,\%$, but that adder is calibrated ex post on this
network and does not transfer (S\ref{subsec:generalis}). The loss-aware node-resolved levels
(\Lone, \Lthree, \Lsix) reach regret $\approx$ bias with no calibration, as expected, since with
no ignored loss to top up the forward-minus-economic residual is a near-constant pump offset, so
the decision-relevant content is the copperplate's asymmetry rather than this near-equality.
Temperature propagation (\Ltwo) is forward-evaluated rather than solved (S2.4, S4.7), its
free-variable form being degenerate. The sign of the
copperplate regret is invariant across three shortfall-pricing schemes (topping up at
the marginal unit, at the peak unit, and penalising unmet demand at the dump price, giving
$+46$/$+61$/$+832$\,\% regret) and across the demand-disaggregation rule
($<0.3$\,pp), so it is not an artefact of either assumption. Physical deliverability is
checked directly: loss-aware schedules incur zero velocity, differential-pressure or
unmet-demand violations under the forward model. This is the decision-relevant reading of
fidelity that model-to-model bias alone cannot give: use the simplest model for
scheduling only if it is loss-aware; never a copperplate.

\subsection{Robustness to a flexible supply temperature}
\label{subsec:tsup}
%% <<DRAFTED:TSUP>> Pillar-2 robustness (tsup_sensitivity.csv). Stress-tests the null
%% against the lever most likely to break it. Method note lives in S2.4 (forward, not a
%% re-solve, because free T_sup is bilinear = MIQP-intractable, same as NL-ref).
%% ---- DRAFTED (author: polish voice) ----
The station-hydraulics null and the loss-dominance result are established with the plant
supply temperature fixed to an outdoor-temperature heating curve. Because a modern operator
may instead lower the supply temperature to cut losses, we stress-test both against that
lever. A free supply temperature is bilinear in the dispatch (demand $=\dot m\,(T_\text{sup}
-T_\text{ret})$) and thus MIQP-intractable, so, consistently with our treatment of the
nonlinear reference, we evaluate it forward rather than re-solve: holding demand and the
return temperature fixed, we lower the plant supply temperature and let the validated forward
model recompute losses, flows and pumping. Lowering the supply temperature reduces trunk
losses but shrinks $\Delta T$, so the flow, the Darcy--Weisbach pumping and the pipe velocity
rise. The cost-optimal reduction is \textbf{17.5}\,K, worth
\textbf{6\,220}\,EUR\,yr$^{-1}$ ($\approx\textbf{4.6}$\,\% of annual
operating cost, valuing the recovered loss at the marginal unit, an upper bound). Two things
follow, both reinforcing the paper's thesis. First, the entire saving is a \emph{loss} effect
(the loss cost falls while pumping rises to partly offset it), so loss visibility, not
routing, remains the operative lever even when the supply temperature is free. Second, the
optimum is set by \emph{hydraulics}: the pump energy rises from \textbf{7.9} to
\textbf{32.3}\,MWh (a factor of four) as the temperature falls, and the pipe
velocity limit binds at a \textbf{20.0}\,K reduction. Hydraulics thus move
from negligible \emph{cost} under a fixed heating curve to the binding \emph{constraint} under
a flexible one: a boundary that qualifies, without overturning, the station-hydraulics null.

\subsection{Linearisation error and transport delay, separated}
\label{subsec:linearisation}
%% <<DRAFTED:LINEARISATION>> (author: polish; numbers: T2P3 138066 vs T2P1 136142 -> +1.4%

Each of the extended phenomena is isolated and, in this regime, each moves the dispatch little.
Trunk pressure and pumping, added to the node-resolved baseline and solved to a $0.01$\,\%
gap, raise cost by about $1.4$\,\%, the pumping the baseline omits, with the ordering
unchanged. Temperature propagation, forward-evaluated as described in Section~\ref{subsec:evaluator}
because its free-variable form is non-convex and degenerate, reduces total network loss by about
$2$\,\%, a few tenths of a percent of cost. Transport delay, a linear time shift, is smaller
still. The linearisation error itself, the gap between the piecewise-linear levels and the
native nonlinear physics, we quantify by \emph{solving} a nonlinear reference, not only by
bounding it. Fixing the mixed-integer schedule from the piecewise-linear model and re-solving the
continuous problem under native (non-linearised) temperature propagation, a non-convex quadratic
programme with the heat-loss bilinearities restored, gives, on a representative winter and autumn
week, a cost change of \textbf{-0.15}\,\% and \textbf{-0.33}\,\%,
each converged to within \textbf{0.03}\,\% of its global optimum. The sign is
informative: it is \emph{negative}, so the piecewise linearisation slightly over-states cost and
the true physics is marginally cheaper; the magnitude stays below half a percent, corroborating
the exact-decomposition bound (the friction and station-pressure terms enter at the
few-hundredths-of-a-percent level and partly cancel). Two limitations of this solved reference are
themselves results. The same re-solve over the full year does not converge: the non-convex
programme finds no incumbent within the solver budget, which is why the reference is evaluated on
representative weeks rather than globally. And a low-load summer week is \emph{infeasible} under
native physics with the linearised schedule fixed: at summer flows the piecewise temperatures the
optimiser relied on cannot actually be delivered, the same physical-deliverability failure that the
decision-regret evaluation exposes (Section~\ref{subsec:evaluator}). We report each effect against
its rigorous optimality bound and do not claim any the attained tolerance cannot resolve; where a
bound does not support a point estimate we say that no effect can be demonstrated, which is a
result rather than a failure. Across all of them the message is the one the decomposition
already gave: everything beyond loss visibility is a fraction of the loss effect.

\subsection{Secondary finding: generalisability and out-of-sample prediction}
\label{subsec:generalis}
%% <<DRAFTED:SYNTH-RESULTS>> SECONDARY finding -- subordinate to the two pillars. ANOVA and
%% regression on the balanced factorial, fitted separately for loss_main, topo_main and
%% regret (this part is the GENERALISATION of Pillar 1 and can stay with it). The a-priori
%% estimator is the demotable piece: fitted on Memmingen + a TRAINING subset of the
%% synthetic factorial and tested on HELD-OUT synthetic networks WITHOUT refitting
%% (Shape A -- NOT Stadtbach), whose pipe lengths lie beyond the fitted range; the
%% parameterised-L4 point extends it to station resolution. Report the outcome honestly.
%% ---- DRAFTED (author: polish voice; ANOVA table from synth CSV) ----
The decomposition generalises. Across the 135 synthetic networks the loss main effect
accounts for a median \textbf{100.0}\,\% of the cost gap and the topology main
effect stays within $\pm$0.6\,\% on every network of 5\,km trunk length or more, never exceeding 2.4\,\% even on the shortest, where the entire gap is below 6\,\% of cost, over the full range of
node counts, pipe lengths, demand heterogeneity and storage sizes; a variance decomposition
attributes essentially all of the between-network variation in the gap to loss and almost none
to topology. The same data expose why the lumped-loss shortcut does not transfer. The loss
\emph{burden}, the share of cost the adder must reproduce, ranges from
\textbf{3.2}\,\% to \textbf{81.2}\,\% across the factorial, driven
chiefly by pipe length; a single frozen adder is by construction correct for only one point in
that range, and even the most transferable choice mis-estimates the burden by a mean of
\textbf{23.5} and up to \textbf{40.1} percentage points of cost
when carried to another network. This is the quantitative form of the non-transfer that makes
the node-resolved model, which computes the loss endogenously, the transferable one. Finally,
an a-priori estimator of the cost gap from observable network properties (pipe length, node
count, demand heterogeneity and storage) fitted on the synthetic networks up to 15\,km trunk
length (train $R^2\approx0.999$) predicts the held-out longer networks without refitting, an
extrapolation beyond the fitted range. It predicts the 30\,km networks to within a couple of
percentage points and under-predicts at the extreme 50\,km extrapolation (held-out mean absolute
percentage error $\approx14$\,\%; Table~S6, Supplementary Material). We report this outcome as given
(extrapolation degrades at the extreme, as one would expect) since a committed-in-advance
prediction, whether it succeeds or fails, is more informative than a post-hoc fitted curve.

The estimator above is a fitted regression on observable properties; the same physics yields a
sharper, parameter-free tool. If the node-resolved model differs from a copperplate only in that
it additionally generates the network loss, and cost scales with heat produced, then the burden a
copperplate misses is $b=\lambda/(1+\lambda)$, where the dimensionless \emph{loss number}
$\lambda=\text{annual loss}/\text{annual demand}$ is computable a priori from the pipe inventory
alone, $\lambda=\big(\sum_p U^{s}_p L_p (T_{\text{sup}}-T_g)+\sum_p U^{r}_p L_p
(T_{\text{ret}}-T_g)\big)\,\cdot 8760/\text{demand}$, without solving any optimisation. This
zero-parameter curve reproduces the measured burden across all \textbf{136} points (the
135 synthetic networks and Memmingen, $\lambda$ spanning
\textbf{0.03}--\textbf{1.7}) with $R^2=\textbf{0.87}$ and a mean
absolute error of \textbf{6.7} percentage points; a one-parameter calibration that
absorbs the residual constant topology term raises this to $R^2=\textbf{0.96}$. Memmingen
($\lambda=\textbf{0.12}$) sits on the curve, its \textbf{11}\,\% predicted
burden close to the \textbf{15}\,\% measured. The residual few points are
informative rather than error: they are the topology main effect, which the rule by
construction does not contain, together with the accounting difference of
Section~\ref{subsec:costacct}, so the rule under-predicts by about the size of the term it
omits, which is what a correctly specified first-principles bound should do. The practical
consequence is a
one-line screening rule, plotted as a nomogram in Figure~\ref{fig:rule}: a planner computes
$\lambda$ from the network geometry and reads off whether a copperplate suffices ($\lambda$ small,
$b\lesssim10$\,\%), whether a calibrated loss adder is adequate, or whether the nodes must be
resolved, turning the paper's central finding into an a-priori design criterion rather than a
result that requires the full model to discover.

The decomposition is not an artefact of a single modelling choice: loss dominance survives every

\subsection{Implications for modelling practice}
%% <<DRAFTED:IMPLICATIONS>> DRAFTED (author: polish voice). Rewrite of v1 S6.2.
%% ---- DRAFTED ----
The results translate into concrete guidance for building district-heating dispatch models.
Zeroth, and most concretely: the decision can be made before any model is built. Memmingen has a loss number of $\lambda=0.12$ from its pipe inventory alone, which the design rule converts to a predicted copperplate error of 11\,\%, above the threshold at which a lumped representation is safe, and therefore an instruction to resolve the nodes. The measured error is 15\,\%. A planner reaches that conclusion from pipe lengths, insulation classes and an annual demand figure, without solving anything. First, what a model must capture is the network loss, not the network graph. A per-node or
per-link loss term recovers almost all of the bias that separating a copperplate from a
node-resolved model reveals, whereas resolving the graph without losses recovers almost none
of it; a modeller on a budget should spend it on loss representation before spatial detail.
Second, the tempting shortcut of a single lumped loss adder is safe only where its coefficient
is already known for the network and operating regime at hand: calibrated on one configuration
it mis-estimates the loss on another by tens of percentage points of cost, so it is a
diagnostic, not a substitute for a model that computes the loss endogenously. Third, and most
practically, estimation accuracy and control adequacy are different requirements and should be
stated separately. A loss-ignoring model can be used for neither (it both misprices and
misschedules), but the general lesson is sharper: a model may be adequate for scheduling
(small regret, deliverable) while being unfit for cost forecasting, CO$_2$ accounting or tariff
setting (large bias), and a modeller should say which use a given fidelity level supports rather
than reporting a single ``accuracy''. Fourth, fidelity should be claimed only to the resolution
at which it can be validated: a model resolved below its metering cannot have its extra detail
verified, so for most operators the node or zone level is the finest \emph{defensible} level,
not merely the cheapest. Finally, the physics beyond losses (trunk pressure, station-resolved
hydraulics, and even a flexible supply temperature) changes dispatch cost by little and
dispatch decisions by less in the setting studied; its main role is as a feasibility
\emph{constraint} (velocity, differential pressure) rather than a driver of the schedule, so it
belongs in a model as bounds to be respected, not as detail to be optimised over.

\subsection{Limitations}
%% <<DRAFTED:SCOPE-CEILING>> ---- DRAFTED ----
The negative results, that extended physics changes decisions little, are conditional on
the regime studied, and we state that plainly. Fixed capacities, a fixed heating curve,
precomputed heat-pump performance, parameterised losses and hourly resolution together limit how
much the extended physics \emph{can} move a dispatch decision; a setting with optimised supply
temperature, sub-hourly transients, demand-side flexibility or joint sizing could give hydraulics
and temperature more room to matter, and we have only bounded the first of these (a flexible
supply temperature, forward-evaluated). We argue, however, that this regime is not a corner case
but the standard planning setting the paper addresses (fixed-asset dispatch on a radial,
centrally-supplied network at hourly resolution), so the conclusions apply where such models are
actually used, and the boundaries above mark exactly where a modeller should re-examine them.
A second, data-driven limitation concerns validation. The network measurements predate the heat
pump, electrode boiler and storage, so while the transport physics those data validate is
asset-independent, the dispatch of the new units is modelled with standard component relations
and checked for plausibility rather than validated against post-upgrade metering. Because our
conclusions are comparative (bias and regret between fidelity levels on a fixed portfolio,
corroborated on the synthetic factorial), they do not rest on a measurement-accurate absolute
schedule; but a post-upgrade measurement campaign validating the dispatch interactions directly
would be the natural next step, and we flag it as such rather than claim it here.

\section{Hydraulic cross-check against a nonlinear pipe-flow solver}\label{app:pandapipes}
%% <<DRAFTED:PANDAPIPES-APP>> (author: polish + add the per-pipe comparison figure/table)
%% ---- DRAFTED ----
As an independent check on the linearised trunk hydraulics, the same network, flows and pipe
geometry were solved with \texttt{pandapipes}, a nonlinear steady-state pipe-flow solver, and
the resulting trunk pressure drops compared pipe by pipe. The two agree to better than
$0.007$\,bar across the trunk, confirming that the piecewise-linear pressure model used in the
optimisation is not the source of the low pumping-energy estimate; the low pumping energy
follows from the network's compactness and heavily oversized pumps (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}),
not from a hydraulic modelling error. The station-internal and service-lateral pressure terms,
which \texttt{pandapipes} does not resolve, are added from the manufacturer datasheets and the
DXF-reconstructed lateral geometry.
